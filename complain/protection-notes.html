Bessent: stated residence, policy intent, and agency action

Records. The publisher’s unofficial November 13 CNBC transcript excerpts record Scott Bessent attributing his move to Secret Service instructions. In the late Iran exchange of CNBC’s November 20 transcript, he confirms he was still living in military housing that day and says Operation Economic Outcast would continue. The opening paragraphs of Treasury’s November 23 release announce specific additional sanctions under that initiative; it also quotes Bessent supporting the policy.

What this establishes and what it does not. These are dated statements and a dated agency action. The interview is evidence of what Bessent said, not independent corroboration of all the premises of his security explanation. The agency release does not establish his residence on its date, or that he personally signed or authorized the measures. A protective arrangement may let legitimate work continue. These sources do not resolve the merits or effects of the policy, the causes of civilian hardship, or whether housing materially enabled it.

Revisit if: a reliable correction changes the chronology; a primary record documents individual authorization; or independent evidence establishes civilian effects or a concrete connection with the protective arrangement.

Minab: an investigative finding, not an individual judgment

Record. The September 21, 2026 report of the UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran, A/HRC/63/61, describes the February 28 strike and casualties at paragraphs 92–98. Paragraph 124 identifies failure to take feasible target-verification precautions. Paragraphs 125–126 explain the mission’s reasoning and its reasonable-grounds conclusion that the U.S.-commanded strike amounted to a war crime. Paragraph 129 discusses responsibility and remedies. Paragraphs 127–128 address a separate strike in Lamerd. The link is a public mirror of the 18-page UN report.

What this establishes and what it does not. The conclusion is the mission’s finding on the record available to it, not an individual criminal judgment. The mission considered statements by senior U.S. leaders, including the Secretary of Defense, but did not determine his individual criminal responsibility. Its evidence at finalization did not include U.S. responses to its requests or a full public disclosure of the U.S. investigation. Military targeting is separate from the Treasury measures discussed in the chronology example.

Revisit if: an operative finding, substantive U.S. response, authenticated targeting record, or new evidence bears on attribution, precautions, or remedies.

Prevention: a request for answers is not evidence of change

Record. Representative Jason Crow’s October 13, 2026 release and reproduced congressional letter ask, in question 3, for Minab investigation findings and, in question 5, for details of any targeting policies implemented since Minab. Question 2 asks about the independence of the separate Kuhestak investigation. The office’s full reproduced text was accessible; the linked signed PDF was not verified in this check.

What this establishes and what it does not. The letter establishes the request. The companion’s test is whether the department can show that target verification changed and whether reviewers outside the operational chain can inspect evidence that the change is used. That is a proposed test, not an assertion that reforms were implemented, that such an inspection has occurred, or that families have received a remedy. This check did not establish a later substantive response; it is not a claim that no response exists.

Revisit if: an official answer, inspectable revised practice, independently documented implementation, or evidence of relief becomes available.

Blanche: an individual act and the limits of a civil finding

Records. The U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York October 26, 2026 opinion, pp. 9–10, 24, 48–50 and 51–55, records Todd Blanche’s May 26 ratification memorandum, holds the Comey removal unlawful, and finds the attempted statutory ratification ineffective. The opinion rejects the government’s statutory and Article II arguments and reserves the separate First Amendment claim. The remedy described in the opinion concerns retroactive reinstatement and backpay through December 20, 2025. This note describes that dated ruling, not the case’s current disposition.

Chronology limit. NewsNation reporting republished by AOL in November 2026 placed his move to military housing in early 2026. That is secondary reporting of an approximate interval, not an established move date or proof of uninterrupted residence. The court opinion establishes an individual act and judicial review; it does not verify the housing timeline, establish that the arrangement enabled the act, or decide criminal guilt.

Revisit if: the underlying memorandum, more precise primary chronology, or a later order, appeal, stay, or documented compliance changes what can be said.
