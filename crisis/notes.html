Why Does Everything Feel Like a Crisis? Source Notes

How to read these notes: each note names the kind of evidence (official definition, guidance, randomized experiment, observational study) and the limit that matters most. 1. Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity

United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (2017). "Disaster risk." Sendai Framework terminology. Official definition

  • Kind: Official conceptual definition. Page inspected.
  • Supports: Disaster risk is defined as potential loss of life, injury or damaged assets, determined as a function of hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity.
  • Limits: A definition, not a study of news or distress. Applying it to everyday information about problems is the guide's own extension. It gives no measure of how much anyone's distress comes from material conditions rather than information.

2. Negative headline wording and clicks

Robertson, C. E., Pröllochs, N., Schwarzenegger, K., Pärnamets, P., Van Bavel, J. J., & Feuerriegel, S. (2023). "Negativity drives online news consumption." Nature Human Behaviour, 7, 812–822. DOI · Accepted manuscript (PDF)

  • Kind: Analysis of randomized headline tests from the Upworthy Research Archive, run January 2013 to April 2015. Accepted manuscript inspected.
  • Design: In each test, readers were randomly shown one of several complete headline versions for the same story. The main analysis used 12,448 tests, 53,699 headlines and over 205 million impressions.
  • Supports: Versions with a higher share of negative words tended to get more clicks. For an average-length headline, their model associated one additional negative word with about a 2.3% relative increase in click-through (not 2.3 percentage points; average click-through was about 1.4%). This is a model-estimated association across whole headlines, not the measured effect of inserting one word.
  • Limits: Whole headline versions were randomized, not individual words inserted, so wording differences come bundled with other differences. One publisher with a distinctive style, in 2013–2015. Clicks are not anxiety, belief or importance. Word-level emotion was scored with dictionaries. In the emotion-specific analysis, fear words were associated with fewer clicks and sadness words with more, which argues against "fear always sells." The study says nothing about individual journalists' motives.

3. Ease of recall and judgments of frequency

Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1973). "Availability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probability." Cognitive Psychology, 5(2), 207–232. DOI · Article PDF (third-party copy)

  • Kind: Original laboratory experiments, largely with student participants. Article inspected.
  • Supports: In the famous-names study, participants heard 39 names: 19 famous people of one sex and 20 less famous people of the other. Of 99 participants asked which sex was more frequent, 80 chose the sex with the more famous (and less numerous) names. Recall was also better for famous names.
  • Limits: The study did not involve news or social media. The authors also describe availability as often a valid cue, since frequent events are usually easier to recall. It does not show that vivid memories are generally wrong or that any reader's concern is mistaken.

4. Individual variation in bodily responses to news

Soroka, S., Fournier, P., & Nir, L. (2019). "Cross-national evidence of a negativity bias in psychophysiological reactions to news." Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(38), 18888–18892. DOI · PubMed Central

  • Kind: Laboratory study of physiological responses to video news. Article inspected through PubMed Central and the publisher's full text; supplementary sampling information could not be retrieved during the final review.
  • Design: 1,156 participants in 17 countries each watched seven BBC World News video stories in random order while skin conductance and heart-rate variability were recorded.
  • Supports: On average, negative stories produced stronger physiological activation than positive ones, but with considerable individual variation; country explained little of the variation.
  • Limits: These are bodily responses, not anxiety, belief or clinical symptoms. These laboratory results do not establish nationally representative estimates. The effect was not statistically significant in every country, and story tone was not isolated from every other difference between stories. The study does not show a universal response to bad news.

5. Coverage exposure, stress symptoms, worry and later media use

Thompson, R. R., Jones, N. M., Holman, E. A., & Silver, R. C. (2019). "Media exposure to mass violence events can fuel a cycle of distress." Science Advances, 5(4), eaav3502. DOI · PubMed Central · UC eScholarship

  • Kind: Longitudinal observational survey of a US national panel, with oversamples near Boston and New York. Full text inspected through a PDF reproduction; the PubMed Central page returned a browser check during this pass, and the eScholarship page was used for the abstract.
  • Design: Four waves from 2013 to 2016: two to four weeks after the Boston Marathon bombing (4,675 respondents), about 6 months later, around the second anniversary, and starting 5 days after the 2016 Pulse nightclub shooting. Analyses included 4,165 people who completed at least two waves. Model specification used 2,450 complete cases; the final model was fitted using full-information maximum likelihood to the 4,165-person analysis sample.
  • Supports: Reported bombing-related media exposure predicted stress symptoms at 6 months; those symptoms predicted worry about future events at 2 years; that worry predicted more media use and acute stress after the Pulse shooting.
  • Limits: Exposure was self-reported and not assigned, so the ordering over time is consistent with a feedback cycle but does not establish one. Recall, attrition (the remaining sample skewed older, wealthier and more educated) and unmeasured differences remain concerns. Stress symptoms are not diagnoses. This is the same cohort as Holman, Garfin & Silver (2014), "Media's role in broadcasting acute stress following the Boston Marathon bombings," PNAS 111(1), 93–98 (DOI), so the two papers are not independent confirmations. The 2014 paper was not re-inspected in this pass.
  • Editorial note: The title's word "fuel" is stronger than the observational design alone supports; the guide says "consistent with a cycle." The paper's own limitations paragraph does not dwell on causal inference, so this qualification is the guide's reasoning.

6. Brief exposure to pandemic content and immediate mood

Buchanan, K., Aknin, L. B., Lotun, S., & Sandstrom, G. M. (2021). "Brief exposure to social media during the COVID-19 pandemic: Doom-scrolling has negative emotional consequences, but kindness-scrolling does not." PLOS ONE, 16(10), e0257728. Full text · DOI

  • Kind: Two randomized online experiments. Article inspected.
  • Design: The reported analyses covered three conditions: COVID-19 information, COVID-19 acts of kindness, or a no-content control. An additional amusement condition was included but not reported in these analyses. Study 1 analyzed 299 participants across the three conditions; the two content groups viewed a live Twitter feed for at least two minutes. Study 2 analyzed 603 participants across the three conditions; the two content groups watched a 4–4.5 minute YouTube reaction video. Control participants received no content and went on to the outcome measures.
  • Supports: Compared with no information, pandemic content lowered positive affect in both studies and lowered optimism in Study 2 only.
  • Limits: Pandemic content did not significantly increase negative affect compared with control in either study. Positive and negative affect are distinct measures. Convenience samples, specific content and immediate measures; no evidence about lasting effects, anxiety disorders or a safe amount of news.

7. A feed-ranking experiment with mixed outcomes

Gauthier, G., Hodler, R., Widmer, P., & Zhuravskaya, E. (2026). "The political effects of X's feed algorithm." Nature, 652, 416–423. Published online 18 February 2026. Article · DOI

  • Kind: Preregistered randomized field experiment, run independently of the platform. Article inspected.
  • Design: Fieldwork July–September 2023, not 2026. Active US users recruited through YouGov were assigned to the algorithmic or chronological feed for about seven weeks; 6,043 completed the baseline survey and 4,965 the follow-up. Compliance was mostly self-reported.
  • Supports: Moving chronological-feed users onto the algorithmic feed raised engagement and shifted some policy priorities and current-event views in a conservative direction. Moving algorithmic-feed users to chronological did not produce comparable attitude changes. Neither switch had a statistically significant effect on partisanship, affective polarization or subjective wellbeing.
  • Limits: One platform, one period, compensated participants. A nonsignificant result is not proof of no effect. The study did not measure a sense of constant crisis. Its 2023 findings say nothing about the platform's configuration today.

8. Communicating risk with numbers and uncertainty

US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2026). "Understanding and Applying Risk Communication Principles." Displayed date September 17, 2026. Official guidance

  • Kind: Practice guidance for communicators. Page inspected.
  • Supports: Acknowledge uncertainty and say when new information is expected; pair words like "low chance" with numbers; use the same denominator when comparing risks; give absolute rather than only relative risk; consider audience context and involve people with lived experience.
  • Limits: The guidance is written for communicators, not readers. Turning it into five reader questions, and the wording "out of how many, over what period, among whom," are the guide's choices. Nothing here shows the questions reduce anxiety or improve decisions.

9. US flood watches and warnings

US National Weather Service. "Flood Warning vs. Watch." Official guidance · US National Weather Service. "During a Flood." Official guidance

  • Kind: Public safety guidance. Both pages inspected; no publication dates shown.
  • Supports: A flood watch is issued when conditions are favorable for flooding (be prepared). A flood warning is issued when flooding is imminent or occurring (take action). The "During a Flood" page advises moving immediately to higher ground if you are in a flood-prone or low-lying area, evacuating promptly when officials order it, staying out of floodwater, and never driving onto flooded roads or around barricades.
  • Limits: US terms and advice only; other countries and hazards use different systems. The actions in the guide's scenario (higher ground, staying off flooded roads, leaving if told to) are drawn from the "During a Flood" page as examples; which applies depends on where the reader is, and local instructions govern. The point that an unrealized forecast is not by itself dishonest is the guide's reasoning, not a finding from this page.

Sources considered and not used

  • de Berker et al. (2016), Nature Communications 7, on uncertainty and acute stress in a laboratory task. Not needed for the method; not inspected.
  • World Health Organization (2017), emergency risk communication guideline. The full guideline was not retrieved in the research packet or in this pass; not cited.
  • Guess et al. (2023), Science, on Facebook and Instagram feed algorithms. Excluded until its March 2026 erratum (DOI) is checked directly. Exclusion reflects an unresolved verification, not a judgment that the paper is wrong.

What this guide does not claim

It does not estimate how much anyone's distress comes from news, algorithms, attention or material conditions. It does not claim the world is getting better or worse overall, that the present is uniquely frightening, or that any group of people is more fearful than another. It does not offer a validated intervention or a recommended daily amount of news.
