# Complain or Constrain? — Notes and Verification

*Research and source inspection: 9 October 2026. Draft for review, not publication approval.*

[Read the draft](complain-or-constrain.md). [Editorial note and suggested repository links](editorial-note.md).

## Scope and method

The article separates experience, causal explanation, and proposed remedy. Its working definitions and six questions are editorial tools, not a validated psychological test or a universal legal test. “Proportionate” and “less restrictive” describe practical reasoning unless a particular legal standard is expressly identified.

Sources below were opened and their relevant passages inspected unless a limitation is stated. A successful request alone was not treated as verification: some pages returned challenge pages or incorrect material and were discarded. Court opinions were read in Cornell Legal Information Institute transcriptions, the National Archives transcript, or (for Ward) Justia’s transcription and hosted original judicial PDF; these are primary judicial texts hosted by secondary or archival publishers, not interviews with participants or independent re-investigation of trial facts. Facts recited by courts are identified as such. Supreme Court syllabuses are summaries, not part of the Court’s opinion; underlying opinion passages were also checked for the central claims.

The selection is deliberately U.S.-heavy and legal, with philosophical context from European and Indian thinkers. It does not establish global prevalence, balanced partisan incidence, or that six cases exhaust the available conflicts. Politically balanced means applying the same evidentiary standards, not allocating blame by party. No motive is inferred merely from an unsuccessful claim.

The unnamed scenarios are invented. They are not descriptions of documented incidents. The article is educational, not advice about how a current court would resolve a reader’s dispute.

## Philosophical and empirical sources

<a id="n1"></a>
### 1. Mill: harm, interference, and exclusions

**Source:** John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), especially chapters I and IV; [full text, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm).

**Checked:** Chapter I states that prevention of harm to others is the purpose justifying coercion against a member of a civilized community; the person’s physical or moral good alone is insufficient. The same chapter explicitly excludes children and what Mill calls “backward states of society,” using “barbarians” and endorsing despotism under an asserted improvement condition. Chapter IV discusses duties and interests affecting others.

**Limit:** The article offers an introductory paraphrase, not a comprehensive theory of harm. The colonial qualification is in the primary text; identifying it as a limitation is the article’s historical and moral judgment. Do not describe Mill as applying an unrestricted principle to everyone or as supplying the legal test in all six cases.

<a id="n2"></a>
### 2. Berlin: negative and positive liberty

**Source inspected:** Ian Carter, [“Positive and Negative Liberty,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liberty-positive-negative/), especially sections on Berlin, self-mastery, and the paradox of positive liberty. This is scholarly secondary exposition, not direct inspection of a first edition of Berlin’s 1958 lecture *Two Concepts of Liberty*.

**Checked:** Negative liberty concerns absence of interference; positive liberty concerns self-direction or self-mastery. The discussion explains Berlin’s concern about coercion supposedly serving someone’s higher or true self.

**Limit:** Positive liberty is not synonymous with authoritarianism; the article does not say it is. The distinction is contested and does not map cleanly onto parties. Berlin’s position should not be conflated with Pettit’s.

<a id="n3"></a>
### 3. Wollstonecraft: authority over oneself

**Source:** Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), chapter IV, “Observations on the State of Degradation to Which Woman Is Reduced by Various Causes,” discussion of Rousseau; [full text, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.txt). The chapter location was rechecked against the text during corrections; the earlier draft incorrectly cited chapter V.

**Checked quotation:** “I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves.” The surrounding passage addresses women’s education and an objection that education will reduce women’s power over men. The text also compares objections to educating the poor.

**Limit:** This is a quotation from a historical argument, not evidence that the author endorsed every modern equality claim. No modern dispute is attributed to her.

<a id="n4"></a>
### 4. Ambedkar: political equality and social hierarchy

**Source:** B. R. Ambedkar’s speech to the Constituent Assembly of India, 25 November 1949; [debate transcript, Constitution of India archive](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/debates/25-nov-1949/). Locate the paragraphs beginning “On the social plane” and “On the 26th of January 1950.”

**Checked:** Ambedkar describes “graded inequality,” contrasts political equality with social and economic inequality, and warns of contradictions between one-person-one-vote and unequal social standing. The transcript also discusses caste and fraternity.

**Limit:** This is a modern Indian constitutional intervention, not a claim that all Indian or non-Western traditions share one doctrine. The analogy to equal standing is editorial; the speech is not represented as a prediction of contemporary U.S. conflicts.

<a id="n5"></a>
### 5. Anderson and Pettit: equality and non-domination

**Primary source inspected:** Elizabeth S. Anderson, “What Is the Point of Equality?”, *Ethics* 109(2), January 1999, pp. 287–337; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/233897); [full-text scan hosted by Rutgers](https://www.philosophy.rutgers.edu/joomlatools-files/docman-files/4ElizabethAnderson.pdf). The DOI landing page was inaccessible; the Rutgers PDF was downloaded and text-extracted.

**Checked:** Printed pp. 288–289 describe ending socially imposed oppression and creating a community in which people stand in relations of equality. The article draws on this claim, not on an assertion that Anderson treats all material differences as domination.

**Scholarly secondary source inspected:** Frank Lovett, [“Republicanism,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/republicanism/), including the discussion of republican liberty and Pettit’s account. Pettit’s *Republicanism* (1997) is a foundational bibliographic reference, but the full book was not independently inspected here.

**Checked:** Non-domination differs from actual noninterference; dependence on arbitrary power can compromise freedom even when no intervention currently occurs.

**Limit:** “Arbitrary” has technical interpretations in this literature, not merely “unpleasant.” Anderson and Pettit are not presented as agreeing on a complete theory. Ordinary relationships of authority do not automatically constitute unjust domination.

<a id="n6"></a>
### 6. Status threat research

**Source inspected:** Diana C. Mutz, [“Status threat, not economic hardship, explains the 2016 presidential vote,” *PNAS* 115(19), 2018, E4330–E4339](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5948965/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718155115).

**Checked:** The study analyzes a 2012–2016 panel and supplementary cross-sectional data. Its abstract and analysis associate changes in preference with threats to dominant group status rather than changes in personal financial well-being.

**What this supports:** A specific research example of perceived status threat as politically relevant.

**What it does not establish:** Individual voters’ motives; that every objection to social change reflects status loss; that material hardship is unreal; that subjective perceptions of discrimination prove discrimination; or that the study settles competing explanations of the election. The analysis was read, not reproduced. Its interpretation is the author’s; observational modeling alone does not supply experimental causal identification. The draft deliberately uses “linked” rather than treating the paper’s title as a universal causal conclusion. A broader literature review would be needed to support broader generalizations.

## Case-specific evidence


**Published challenge and response:** Stephen L. Morgan, [“Status Threat, Material Interests, and the 2016 Presidential Vote,” *Socius* 4 (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2378023118788217), reanalyzed Mutz’s released data and disputed the economic-versus-status conclusion. His abstract argues that her cross-sectional analysis cannot effectively estimate their relative importance and that the panel data are consistent with economic interests being at least as important as status threat. These are Morgan’s conclusions, not findings independently reproduced for this guide. Diana Mutz replied in [“Response to Morgan: On the Role of Status Threat and Material Interests in the 2016 Election,” *Socius* 4 (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2378023118808619). The disagreement is a reason to present the original finding as one study’s interpretation, not a settled ranking of explanations.

**Access record for the exchange:** Titles, authors, publication details, DOIs, and abstracts were checked in publisher-deposited Crossref records. The publisher’s full-text pages returned browser challenges. These metadata and abstracts establish the publications and Morgan’s stated challenge; they do not constitute a full independent review of either reanalysis or reply.

<a id="n7"></a>
### 7. Plessy and Brown: imposed exclusion, dismissed injury

**Primary texts inspected:**

- [*Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/163/537), majority discussion at pp. 550–552, particularly p. 551.
- [*Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), National Archives transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/brown-v-board-of-education), particularly opinion pp. 492–495.

**Established record:** Plessy concerns Louisiana railway segregation. The majority says the supposed badge of inferiority arises because the Black population “chooses to put that construction upon it.” Brown concerns public education and holds separate educational facilities inherently unequal, addressing intangible consequences even where physical factors were equal or being equalized.

**Editorial application:** The Plessy majority’s response is a documented dismissal of the injury associated with an imposed racial restriction. Brown illustrates recognition that disadvantage is not exhausted by tangible facilities.

**Limits and contrary context:** These are different cases and settings, not two stages of the same lawsuit. Brown did not instantly end segregation, prescribe every implementation detail in the 1954 opinion, or establish that subjective feelings alone create a constitutional violation. Its decision rested on state-imposed racial segregation in public education. The draft does not treat a modern reader’s discomfort as equivalent to that system.

<a id="n8"></a>
### 8. Bob Jones University: religious exercise and tax advantages

**Primary source inspected:** [*Bob Jones University v. United States*, 461 U.S. 574 (1983), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/461/574), particularly factual history pp. 579–585 and religious exercise analysis pp. 602–605. The opinion includes the consolidated Goldsboro case.

**Established record:** Bob Jones defended restrictions involving interracial dating and marriage on religious grounds. Goldsboro maintained racially discriminatory admissions. The Court upheld the IRS’s denial of tax-exempt treatment and related charitable tax benefits, construing the governing statutory framework in light of fundamental public policy. It said denial would have “a substantial impact” on religious schools’ operations but held that the interest in eliminating racial discrimination in education “substantially outweighs whatever burden” denial imposes on religious exercise (pp. 603–604).

**Editorial application:** Losing a public financial advantage can be costly without its retention being a justified remedy. This illustrates the distinction between protection of belief and entitlement to tax advantages for discriminatory institutional practices.

**Limits:** The draft does not claim a verified quotation calling the decision “persecution,” or that every lost tax exemption is merely loss of privilege. This is a narrower, verified substitute for that requested example category. The Court’s conclusion does not authorize general viewpoint-based discrimination against religious organizations. Justice Rehnquist’s dissent contested statutory authority and Congress’s role; it should not be reduced to endorsement of racial discrimination. The moral assessment of unequal student treatment is distinct from that statutory disagreement.

<a id="n9"></a>
### 9. Japanese American exclusion and incarceration

**Primary retrospective source inspected:** Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, *Personal Justice Denied* (report issued 1982, recommendations 1983), [National Archives collection](https://www.archives.gov/research/aapi/ww2/justice) and [summary PDF](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/japanese-americans/justice-denied/summary.pdf), especially printed pp. 5–8 and 18.

**Established record:** The report traces Executive Order 9066 and the military exclusion and detention measures, involving U.S. citizens and resident aliens of Japanese ancestry. Its findings reject military necessity for the exclusion and identify racial prejudice, war hysteria, and failure of political leadership. The report states that people were deprived of liberty without individual review or probative evidence against them.

**Editorial application:** A genuine wartime threat did not justify ancestry-based restrictions of this scope. Roosevelt’s administration is identified because responsibility matters, not to imply that the policy was uniquely supported by one party.

**Limits:** The report is a later official investigation, not a contemporaneous judicial holding. The article does not claim that no security threats existed, that every person involved had an identical motive, or that every U.S. wartime detention program had the same legal basis. Executive Order 9066 itself did not expressly name Japanese Americans. “Authorized measures that led to” preserves that distinction. The draft avoids the shorthand that *Korematsu* directly upheld every aspect of incarceration.

<a id="n10"></a>
### 10. Snyder: genuine distress and rejected damages

**Primary texts inspected:** [*Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), majority opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/09-751.ZO.html), especially parts I, II, and IV; [Alito dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/09-751.ZD.html).

**Established record:** The majority recounts the funeral picketing, approximately 1,000-foot distance from the church, absence of entry onto church property or disruption, and Snyder’s later viewing of the signs on television. It explicitly acknowledges his anguish. The majority protects the speech against the tort liability at issue, emphasizing content, form, context, and the danger of imposing liability through a subjective “outrageousness” standard.

**Strongest contrary case:** Alito argues that the attack on a private person and his family does not deserve protection merely because it is interspersed with public issues. This is a substantive disagreement about speech and injury, not evidence that either side denied all relevant rights.

**Editorial application:** This supplies the requested case of genuine harm paired with a remedy rejected as an impermissible restriction. The article carefully calls it legally impermissible under the majority’s holding, rather than declaring that Snyder’s request was obviously malicious or that seeking damages is inherently oppressive. A civil damages award is coercive state-backed relief even though it is not a prospective blanket speech ban.

**Limits:** The majority expressly describes its holding as narrow. Threats, trespass, defamation, captive-audience questions, and different protest restrictions are not all decided by this case. The separate online “epic” was not properly before the Supreme Court on the issues presented; it must not be smuggled into the majority’s protected-picketing holding. The dissent discusses it. No new damages amount is asserted in the draft.

<a id="n11"></a>
### 11. Holt: justified protection requiring institutional change

**Primary source inspected:** [*Holt v. Hobbs*, 574 U.S. 352 (2015), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/13-6827), majority’s treatment of contraband, identification, and alternatives.

**Established record:** A Muslim prisoner sought a half-inch beard accommodation. The Court held the restriction unlawful as applied under the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act (RLUIPA). The Department had not demonstrated that its policy was the least restrictive means of furthering its asserted compelling interests. Searches and paired photographs were among the alternatives discussed.

**Editorial application:** A person who may initially appear to be telling officials how to run an institution can instead be seeking justified protection of their own practice. This is the strongest explicit reversal of the “asking others to change equals control” shortcut. It also supplies the accommodation example.

**Limits:** Prison safety is legitimate. The holding is about this policy and proposed accommodation, not an exemption from all prison rules or a finding that religious accommodations can never harm third parties. “Why should one prisoner get to dictate prison rules?” is an invented reader reaction, not a quotation from officials. RLUIPA’s legal standard is not presented as the governing rule for ordinary workplace or neighbor disputes.

<a id="n12"></a>
### 12. Lukumi: substantiated discrimination against a minority religion

**Primary source inspected:** [*Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520 (1993), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/508/520), especially the ordinances, neutrality/general applicability analysis, and less restrictive measures at pp. 535–546.

**Established record:** The ordinances restricted Santería animal sacrifice while exempting or leaving outside their scope many comparable secular animal killings. The Court found constitutional defects in the laws’ targeting and lack of general applicability. Animal welfare and public health were acknowledged as legitimate concerns; the mismatch between those concerns and the rules mattered.

**Editorial application:** A discrimination claim can be substantiated even when the practice at issue is unfamiliar or troubling to many readers. This also illustrates comparison of like risks rather than comparison of emotional reactions to groups.

**Limits:** Do not say the ruling established that animal sacrifice is harmless, created an unlimited religious exemption, or invalidated all animal-welfare law. Not every part of the discussion of legislative motive commanded the same joining justices; the draft relies on the laws’ design and the constitutional result rather than attributing a single psychological intent to all officials or justices. Unpopular is a contextual description, not a quantified opinion-poll finding.

### 13. Ward: defense rights, individual evidence, and potentially prejudicial inferences

**Primary source inspected in full:** *State of Tennessee v. Samuel Ward, Jr.*, No. W2025-01186-CCA-R3-CD, Tennessee Court of Criminal Appeals, filed **5 October 2026**, Timothy L. Easter, J., joined by Robert W. Wedemeyer and Matthew J. Wilson. [Judicial text on Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/tennessee/court-of-criminal-appeals/2026/w2025-01186-cca-r3-cd.html); [original opinion PDF hosted by Justia](https://cases.justia.com/tennessee/court-of-criminal-appeals/2026-w2025-01186-cca-r3-cd.pdf?ts=1791322347). The PDF’s title page, date, docket, and opinion text were checked; this was not reliance on an AI case summary. The [official Tennessee PDF address](https://www.tncourts.gov/sites/default/files/OpinionsPDFVersion/W2025-01186-CCA-R3-CDOPN.pdf) returned a browser challenge rather than the document.

**Rules used:** The opinion quotes Tennessee Rules of Evidence **401** (relevance) and **403** (exclusion where probative value is substantially outweighed by specified dangers), pp. 7–10. The article uses those quotations and the court’s application. Separate requests to the official [Rule 401](https://www.tncourts.gov/rules/rules-evidence/401) and [Rule 403](https://www.tncourts.gov/rules/rules-evidence/403) pages returned browser challenges; no independently inspected consolidated rules edition is claimed. The opinion also discusses Tennessee Code § 39-11-611 and authorities concerning the right to present a defense. It expressly says that right is not absolute.

**Reporting inspected:** s. baum, [“Killing of Woman May Have Been Justifiable Because She Was Trans, Tennessee Court Rules,” *Erin in the Morning*, 9 October 2026](https://www.erininthemorning.com/p/killing-of-woman-may-have-been-justifiable). This is reporting from an LGBTQ-focused publication with an advocacy perspective, linking the original opinion and reporting community concerns. Its headline is not adopted as a statement of the holding. The court ordered a new trial, not a finding of justified killing. A [PinkNews report](https://www.thepinknews.com/2026/10/09/paris-lee-new-trial-samuel-ward-jr-tennessee-court/) was located in search but its full text was not successfully retrieved; it supplies no independent verification here.

**Evidence and arguments, separated:**

- **Procedural history and result (pp. 1, 12–13, 17):** Ward was convicted of voluntary manslaughter and sentenced to six years. The appellate court vacated the conviction and remanded for a new trial because of the exclusion of evidence. It also rejected the insufficiency challenge, holding that a rational jury could convict on the trial evidence. These conclusions address different questions and are not inconsistent.
- **Defense account (pp. 2, 4–5):** The State’s motion averred a sexual relationship lasting “at least two years” and Ward’s knowledge of Lee’s sex assigned at birth (pp. 1–2). The proffer of his intended testimony likewise involved fear because he knew that fact, and the appellate court treated it as known to him (pp. 5, 11). The relationship length is the State’s averment, not a separately verified finding. Ward claimed self-defense and gave accounts of Lee pointing a firearm and his shooting while falling down stairs. The opinion recounts inconsistencies and forensic evidence. His fear and account must be attributed, not presented as independently established facts. At the hearing about testifying, he said he wanted to tell “the whole truth”; he ultimately did not testify. The State’s proffer stipulation concerning what he would say was not a concession that his fear was reasonable or the shooting justified.
- **Individual characteristics and physical evidence (pp. 4–5):** The autopsy recorded Lee as five feet eleven inches and 262 pounds. The opinion recounts six gunshot wounds, their trajectories, firearm testing, and seven cartridge cases attributed to Ward’s gun and one to Lee’s. These are case-specific data, not direct measurements of Lee’s strength. Their significance and competing accounts remained matters for evaluation; the article does not decide who fired first or reconstruct the shooting.
- **Trial court’s concerns (pp. 2–3, 5–6):** The State sought exclusion as irrelevant and potentially prejudicial. The judge expressly ruled sex irrelevant in this situation, emphasized that a firearm is deadly irrespective of sex, and wanted to avoid confusion and mistrial. Photographs and the autopsy report were redacted. This is more specific than saying the judge merely prohibited insulting terminology.
- **Appellate reasoning (pp. 7–12):** The court emphasized relevance’s low threshold; relative strength and size as self-defense considerations; general physiological differences; the possibility of bias affecting Ward because of his relationship with Lee; and the court’s conclusion that exclusion “left the jury with the impression that a male defendant killed a female victim” and “misled the jury” (p. 10). It found misapplication of Rule 403 and violation of the right to present a defense, with error not harmless. A summary confined to “judges disagreed about pronouns” would be false.
- **Group inference concern (pp. 8–9):** The court invoked physiological differences without a detailed scientific discussion or cited study in that passage, stating: “A detailed discussion is not necessary for our analysis; it suffices to acknowledge that these differences exist” (p. 8). The article’s caution that a group characteristic does not by itself establish an individual’s capabilities or dangerousness is an analytical criticism, not a contrary scientific study, an assertion that sex-related population differences never exist, or proof of judicial motive.

**Framework fit:** A legitimate right to defend oneself in court can coexist with concern about the inferential use of admitted evidence. Relevance, the weight evidence deserves, and the danger of unfair prejudice are distinct. This example does not establish that Ward’s entire request was unjustified: the appellate court ruled in his favor on exclusion. It is included as a short complication after the six core cases, not a forced binary classification.

**Factual and publication limits:** The complete trial record, unredacted exhibits, underlying briefs, medical history, and evidence outside the opinion were not independently inspected. No finding is made about Lee’s actual strength, Ward’s credibility or guilt on retrial, or the judges’ intentions. **Docket recheck attempted 9 October 2026:** the official Tennessee courts public-case-history entry page returned a browser challenge. The [Appellate Case Search](https://pch.tncourts.gov/) loaded, but submissions for `W2025-01186-CCA-R3-CD` returned the search form without case history. That is not a verified negative search. Subsequent rehearing, discretionary review, or retrial developments remain unconfirmed; no claim is made that no appeal has been filed. The article now states its as-of date and that further appeal is possible. A successful docket check remains due before publication. Do not describe the decision as acquitting Ward, legalizing violence against transgender people, holding identity alone sufficient for self-defense, or establishing that the killing was justified.


## Adversarial checks

- Does compassion become automatic agreement? No: the opening, threat illustration, and Snyder distinguish injury from remedy.
- Does requiring another person to change automatically become domination? No: Holt, accommodation costs, and anti-harassment rules expressly reject that shortcut.
- Does subjective suffering become irrelevant? No: testimony is evidence, support need not await severe injury, and low-burden responses may precede full fact-finding.
- Are legal decisions treated as final moral proof? The draft identifies legal holdings, states its judgments separately, and includes Snyder’s dissent.
- Is a powerful person’s grievance automatically false? No: the text says present burdens need examination and status research cannot diagnose individuals.
- Is the current arrangement presumed neutral? No: question D asks readers to count its existing costs.
- Is partisan symmetry manufactured? No: cases were selected for different reasoning failures, without claims about comparative party prevalence.
- Is a group stereotype substituted for individualized evidence? The opening, threat illustration, and wartime case explicitly challenge that move.

This checklist records editorial reasoning, not experimental validation of the framework or certification that no error remains.
