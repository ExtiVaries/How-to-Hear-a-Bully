# Why Does Everything Feel Like a Crisis?

*A short guide to judging alarming information and choosing what to do. A companion to* How to Hear a Bully.

*Numbered markers link to a separate file, [Source Notes](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md), which lists each source and its limits. You don't need the notes to follow the guide.*

> **If you are in danger now,** or a current emergency warning covers where you are, follow the instructions of local emergency officials first. This guide can wait.

---

## The feeling, and what this guide is for

Some days every alert, headline and group chat seems to carry an emergency. If you know that feeling, this guide is for you. If you don't, that's fine.

The feeling is not a mistake to be corrected. Sometimes it is an accurate response to real danger. Sometimes it comes from hearing about the same event again and again. Often it is a mix, and the mix is hard to sort out from the inside. This guide offers a way to sort it: a few questions that can leave you more worried, less worried, or worried about something different from where you started. The goal is a better-supported judgment and a response you can actually carry out. A calmer reader is not the point.

## Start with what is actually happening

Before asking how news reaches us, ask what is happening to people. Rent goes up, a job ends, a clinic closes, a river rises. These are conditions, not perceptions, and they land unevenly.

Disaster researchers make a useful distinction. Whether a hazard turns into loss depends on more than the hazard itself. It also depends on who and what is exposed, how vulnerable they are, and what capacity they have to cope.[[1]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n1)<a id="r1"></a> The same heavy rain means one thing to a household on high ground with insurance and a car. It means something very different to a family in a basement flat with no car and a relative who needs care. Same headline, different stakes.

Two things follow. First, a person who feels under constant pressure may simply be under constant pressure. No amount of careful news reading repairs unsafe housing or replaces lost income. Second, concern is not only about yourself. Caring what happens to people elsewhere, or to neighbors whose risk is higher than yours, is legitimate even when your own exposure is low.

So the rest of this guide does not argue that problems are imaginary. It takes up a separate question: how information about problems reaches you, and how to judge it.

## What reaches you is selected

What arrives on a screen has passed through many choices: what editors covered, what people shared, what you searched for, which accounts you follow, and how an app ranked the results. Some of that selection helps. It can bring neglected harm into view and put the most relevant story first.

Selection can also follow attention. One large study examined thousands of real tests run by a single news website between 2013 and 2015, in which different readers were randomly shown different headlines for the same story. On average, the headline versions with more negative words drew somewhat more clicks.[[2]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n2)<a id="r2"></a> That is a measured incentive, but its limits matter. It covers one publisher in one period. Whole headlines were compared, so it links clicks to negative wording without isolating the effect of any single word. It measured clicks, not fear or belief. In a separate analysis, fear-related wording was associated with fewer clicks. And it tells us nothing about any particular journalist's motives or any article's quality.

Selection matters because of how people judge frequency. In a classic experiment, people heard a list of names and then judged whether it held more men or more women. When one sex's names were more famous, most people judged that group to be larger, even though it was slightly smaller.[[3]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n3)<a id="r3"></a> What comes easily to mind can stand in for how often something happens. The shortcut often works, because common things are usually easy to recall. But a vivid story repeated in many places is easy to recall whether or not such events are becoming more common.

The useful distinction is between examples you have encountered and events someone has counted. More visibility can mean more events. It can also mean better reporting of harm that was always there, or one event retold many times.

## Feeling, checking, and checking again

Strong reactions to bad news are real experiences. They are not a malfunction. Fear can signal that something matters to you, but it cannot, by itself, establish how likely or severe a danger is. People also differ a great deal in how strongly their bodies respond to the same news.[[4]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n4)<a id="r4"></a>

Feelings and checking may feed each other. A study followed thousands of American adults for three years after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing. People who reported more exposure to coverage of the bombing later reported more stress symptoms. Those symptoms were followed by more worry about the future, and that worry by heavier media use after a mass shooting in 2016.[[5]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n5)<a id="r5"></a> The pattern is consistent with a cycle. But the study observed people rather than assigning what they watched, so it cannot show that coverage caused the distress, and it cannot tell any one person why they feel as they do.

Experiments can test shorter effects. In two randomized studies during the COVID-19 pandemic, a few minutes of pandemic content lowered people's positive mood compared with seeing nothing, but it did not produce a statistically clear rise in negative feelings.[[6]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n6)<a id="r6"></a> That is narrower than "news makes you anxious," and it says nothing about lasting effects or a safe amount of news.

Different measurements tell different stories. Clicks, pulse, mood, stress symptoms, political opinions and actual danger are separate things. A recent experiment that switched some X users between ranked feeds (posts selected by the platform) and chronological ones (newest first) in 2023 found changes in engagement and some opinions, but no detectable change in wellbeing.[[7]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n7)<a id="r7"></a> Claims that "the algorithm" does everything, or nothing, outrun the evidence.

## Five questions

If there is direct danger, or a credible current warning that applies to you, act on it first. These questions are for concerns that aren't immediate, or for afterward. They are a teaching aid that borrows from public-health guidance on communicating risk; they are not a proven treatment or a danger score.[[8]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n8)<a id="r8"></a>

1. **What happened, and what is being claimed?** Where and when? Separate what was observed from what is alleged, forecast or opinion.
2. **What changed, compared with what?** Is this a new event or a new report about an old one? For a claimed trend, ask: out of how many, over what period, among whom, and did the way things are counted or reported change? A trend that isn't shown is not proof that nothing is wrong.
3. **Who is affected, and how seriously?** Look at who bears the harm, not only the average. An average is not a forecast for you, and people other than you count.
4. **What do we know, and what would change the assessment?** Name what's uncertain and what the next meaningful update would be. Trusted sources can be wrong, and a correction can mean the evidence improved.
5. **What response fits the stakes and the time available?** Weigh how severe, how urgent and how reversible the harm is, and what precautions cost. Options include protecting yourself, preparing, helping someone, acting through institutions or with others, rechecking at a set time, or doing nothing more for now.

Use the same questions on reassuring claims as on alarming ones, and on sources you trust as on sources you don't.

## Practice: a fictional flood

*Everything in this example is invented: the town, the storm, the posts and the numbers.*

After a heavy storm, you see sixteen posts about flooding in your town. Later, a town report says 8 of the town's 1,000 homes flooded. All 8 were among the 80 homes along the river. No comparable records from earlier years are given. The storm is over; these figures describe damage that already happened.

Two claims circulate:

- **Claim A:** "Sixteen posts show that floods here are becoming more frequent."
- **Claim B:** "Only 0.8% of homes flooded, so everyone can stop worrying."

**Claim A** counts posts as if they were floods. Sixteen posts about one storm can add real evidence about that storm, such as which streets were hit, but they describe one event, not sixteen. A trend needs comparable records over time, and none are given, so the trend is unknown. That is not the same as "no problem." The damage happened, and the missing comparison is worth looking for.

**Claim B** gets the arithmetic right and the meaning wrong. Eight out of 1,000 is 0.8%. Eight out of 80 riverside homes is 10%. Both numbers accurately describe this storm, but the town-wide figure hides where the damage fell. Neither number is the chance that a particular home floods next time.

A fitting response depends on where you stand. Riverside households might check their own exposure and preparation. Neighbors might help with cleanup or ask the town about drainage. Someone far from the river might decide nothing more is needed for now.

## When a current warning calls for action

*Also fictional, but built on official US flood alert terms.*

Months later, another storm arrives. The US National Weather Service issues a flood warning that includes your neighborhood. A friend says, "Remember last time? Only 0.8% of homes flooded. Ignore it."

In US usage, a flood watch means conditions favor flooding, so get prepared. A flood warning means flooding is imminent or already happening, so take action.[[9]](why-does-everything-feel-like-a-crisis-notes.md#n9)<a id="r9"></a> The last storm's town-wide share does not establish your safety in this storm, and even then it hid the riverside damage. Follow the warning and the protective instructions of local officials. What that means depends on where you are: it might be moving to higher ground, staying off flooded roads, or leaving if you are told to. Don't stop to work through these questions first.

If the flood doesn't reach you, that alone doesn't make the warning a lie. Forecasts deal in uncertainty. Other countries use other alert terms, so learn your local system, and keep emergency alerts on even if you silence everything else.

## Choosing what to do

Changing how you follow the news is one option, and it can be worth trying. Notice which checks bring new information and which only repeat it. Decide when another look could actually change what you'd do. Pick a few competent sources for the topics that matter to you. Mute nonessential notifications while keeping emergency ones. Treat these as experiments, not rules; nothing here supports a correct daily amount.

Many concerns call for other responses: getting help, protecting your household, preparing, helping a neighbor, pressing a local office to fix something, or joining people already working on the problem. What's possible depends on time, money, transport, health, caregiving and whether institutions have earned your trust. If you can't step away or take a precaution, that is a constraint, not a failure.

Sometimes the honest answer is "I don't know yet." You can stay concerned, take reasonable precautions and decide when to look again. These questions are meant to stay useful wherever they lead, including to the conclusion that things are worse than you thought.
