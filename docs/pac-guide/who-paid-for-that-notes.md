# Source Notes — Who Paid for That?

*Research cutoff: 5 November 2026. These notes support the draft guide, not a complete legal treatise or a finding about any organization’s compliance. Federal rules are emphasized; state law can differ. Dynamic financial figures use the source coverage dates stated below.*

<a id="n1"></a>
### 1. Earmarked contributions and conduits

**Sources:** Federal Election Commission, [Earmarked contributions](https://www.fec.gov/updates/earmarked-contributions/); FEC, [Contributions received through conduits](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/filing-reports/contributions-received-through-conduits/).

**Supports:** An earmarked contribution is directed to a clearly identified candidate through an intermediary or conduit. Reporting must identify the original contributor and conduit under the applicable rules. The contribution always counts against the original donor's limit and also counts against the conduit's limit if the conduit directs or controls the choice of recipient. That attribution rule does not turn one payment into two.

**Limits:** Whether a particular transaction was earmarked must be determined from its filing and facts. Acting as a conduit does not establish coordination over independent spending.

<a id="n2"></a>
### 2. Independent expenditures

**Source:** FEC, [Making independent expenditures](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-independent-expenditures/).

**Supports:** An independent expenditure expressly advocates election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate and is not made in consultation or cooperation with a candidate, campaign, or party. It is not a candidate contribution and is not subject to contribution amount limits, though reporting, disclaimer, and source prohibitions apply.

**Limits:** “Independent” is a legal classification; a public relationship or shared ideology alone does not establish coordination. Coordination depends on the applicable payment, content, and conduct tests.

<a id="n3"></a>
### 3. Leadership PACs

**Sources:** FEC, [Leadership PACs](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/registering-pac/types-nonconnected-pacs/leadership-pacs/); FEC, [Leadership PACs and sponsors description](https://www.fec.gov/campaign-finance-data/leadership-pacs-and-sponsors-description/).

**Supports:** Members of Congress and other political leaders often establish leadership PACs to support candidates for federal and nonfederal office. They remain legally unaffiliated with the sponsor’s principal campaign committee and operate under nonconnected-PAC rules.

**Limits:** Legal separation does not answer every question about political influence, common staff, or permissible relationships; those require transaction- and rule-specific evidence.

<a id="n4"></a>
### 4. Super PACs and unlimited contributions

**Sources:** FEC, [Types of nonconnected PACs](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/registering-pac/types-nonconnected-pacs/); FEC, [Contributions to Super PACs and Hybrid PACs](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/taking-receipts-pac/contributions-to-super-pacs-and-hybrid-pacs/).

**Supports:** Independent-expenditure-only committees may accept unlimited contributions from individuals, corporations, labor organizations, and other political committees for independent political activity. They may not accept money from prohibited sources including foreign nationals and federal contractors.

**Limits:** “Unlimited” refers to amount limits, not freedom from reporting, disclaimer, coordination, or source restrictions.

<a id="n5"></a>
### 5. Section 501(c)(4) organizations

**Sources:** IRS, [Social welfare organizations](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/other-non-profits/social-welfare-organizations); IRS, [Endorsing candidates for public office](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/eo-operational-requirements-endorsing-candidates-for-public-office); FEC, [Who can and cannot contribute to a candidate](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/candidate-taking-receipts/who-can-and-cant-contribute/).

**Supports:** A 501(c)(4) organization may engage primarily in lobbying germane to its social-welfare purposes. It may engage in some political campaign activity so long as political campaign intervention is not its primary activity; political expenditures can be subject to tax. An incorporated 501(c)(4), like other nonprofit corporations, cannot make treasury contributions directly to federal candidates; funds from an eligible separate PAC may be contributed within applicable limits.

**Limits:** The IRS pages do not supply one universal numeric safe harbor for “primary activity.” Particular activities and reporting obligations require fact-specific analysis. Section 501(c)(4) tax status alone does not establish corporate form or resolve all election-law restrictions.

### 6. Researching committees in FEC records

**Sources:** FEC, [How to research PACs, parties and other committees](https://www.fec.gov/introduction-campaign-finance/how-to-research-public-records/pacs-parties-and-other-committees/); FEC, [Browse data](https://www.fec.gov/data/browse-data/).

**Supports:** FEC records provide committee IDs, registration documents, reports, summary figures, itemized receipts, disbursements, and independent-expenditure information.

**Limits:** Reports are filed by regulated entities and can be amended. A filed transaction establishes what was reported, not automatic verification of every underlying fact or legality.

<a id="n7"></a>
### 7. Advertisement disclaimers

**Sources:** FEC, [Advertising and disclaimers](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/advertising-and-disclaimers/); FEC, [Making independent expenditures](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-independent-expenditures/).

**Supports:** Covered political communications must identify the payor and state whether a candidate authorized the communication.

**Limits:** A disclaimer identifies the legal payor, not necessarily every contributor to that payor. Requirements vary by communication type and facts. The FEC page includes a notice that it had not yet been revised for a June 30, 2026 Supreme Court ruling concerning party coordinated-expenditure limits; this guide does not rely on that separate issue.

<a id="n8"></a>
### 8. Public and IRS disclosure of nonprofit donors

**Sources:** IRS, [Contributors’ identities not subject to disclosure](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/public-disclosure-and-availability-of-exempt-organizations-returns-and-applications-contributors-identities-not-subject-to-disclosure); IRS, [Internal Revenue Bulletin 2020-25](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2020-25_IRB); IRS, [Instructions for Schedule B](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i990sb).

**Supports:** Contributor names and addresses on exempt-organization annual returns are generally not public. The 2020 final regulations removed annual Schedule B name-and-address reporting for many exempt organizations other than 501(c)(3) organizations and section 527 political organizations. Affected organizations must retain records and provide them upon a proper IRS request.

**Limits:** Different rules apply to private foundations, section 527 organizations, state regulators, particular election-related reports, and records obtained in investigations. Public anonymity is not the same as absence of an internal record.

<a id="n9"></a>
### 9. Campaign disclosure and *Citizens United*

**Source:** U.S. Supreme Court, [*Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/558bv.pdf), Part IV.

**Supports:** The Court upheld the disclaimer and disclosure provisions at issue, distinguishing them from expenditure ceilings and describing disclosure as providing voters information.

**Limits:** The opinion does not approve every conceivable disclosure requirement. Later cases address other regimes and burdens.

<a id="n10"></a>
### 10. Associational privacy

**Sources:** Legal Information Institute, [*NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449 (1958)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/357/449); U.S. Supreme Court, [*Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 594 U.S. 595 (2021)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-251_p86b.pdf).

**Supports:** Compelled disclosure can burden association, especially where disclosure exposes members or donors to reprisals. The Court invalidated the disclosure regimes at issue in those cases.

**Limits:** Neither case establishes a universal right to anonymous election spending. The constitutional analysis depends on the disclosure rule, governmental interest, tailoring, and burdens.

[Back to the guide](who-paid-for-that.md#why-donor-privacy-exists)

<a id="n11"></a>
### 11. Foreign nationals

**Sources:** FEC, [Foreign nationals](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/foreign-nationals/); FEC, [Who can and cannot contribute to a nonconnected PAC](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/taking-receipts-pac/who-can-and-cant-contribute-nonconnected-pac/).

**Supports:** Foreign nationals may not directly or indirectly make contributions, donations, expenditures, or independent expenditures in connection with U.S. elections; they may not act as conduits or participate in covered election-spending decisions. For individuals, the definition excludes U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents; qualifying green-card holders may contribute. The FEC also states that a surname alone is not a reason to infer foreign nationality.

**Limits:** Citizenship, lawful permanent residence, entity structure, source of funds, and decision-making participation require facts. An address abroad or nonprofit opacity alone does not prove a prohibited source.

<a id="n12"></a>
### 12. Contributions in the name of another

**Sources:** FEC, [Contributions in the name of another are strictly prohibited](https://www.fec.gov/updates/contributions-in-the-name-of-another-are-strictly-prohibited/); FEC, [Who can and cannot contribute to a candidate](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/candidate-taking-receipts/who-can-and-cant-contribute/).

**Supports:** Federal law prohibits making a contribution in another person’s name, knowingly allowing one’s name to be used for another person’s contribution, or knowingly accepting such a disguised contribution. Reimbursement and straw-donor arrangements can violate the rule.

**Limits:** A lawful disclosed conduit forwards an earmarked contribution in the original donor’s name. That is different from a straw donor whose name conceals the true source.

### 13. FEC enforcement routes

**Sources:** FEC, [Enforcing federal campaign finance law](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/); FEC, [Complaints process](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/complaints-process/); FEC, [FEC’s response to possible violations](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/fecs-response-possible-violations/).

**Supports:** FEC matters can arise from report review, audit, sworn complaint, agency referral, or self-submission. Outcomes can include compliance letters, conciliation agreements, and civil penalties.

**Limits:** A complaint is an allegation, not a finding. FEC civil jurisdiction does not replace DOJ criminal jurisdiction or IRS tax administration.

<a id="n14"></a>
### 14. Commission votes and confidentiality

**Sources:** FEC, [Leadership and structure](https://www.fec.gov/about/leadership-and-structure/); FEC, [FEC enforcement programs](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/fec-enforcement-programs/).

**Supports:** At least four commissioner votes are required for official Commission action. If four votes are unavailable at a major enforcement stage, the matter does not proceed to the next stage. As checked on 5 November 2026, the official roster lists Shana M. Broussard and Dara Lindenbaum, with four vacant seats, so the Commission lacks a quorum for those decisions. The FEC generally keeps enforcement matters confidential until closed and later publishes redacted files; complainants and respondents may disclose their own complaints or responses.

**Limits:** The dated roster is not a permanent description of Commission membership. A quorum shortage does not suspend reporting requirements, all staff functions, or other agencies' authority. Failure to proceed can have different causes and does not itself establish that allegations were true or false.

### 17. AIPAC PAC as conduit

**Sources:** FEC candidate filing example, [Alaskans for Dan Sullivan, Schedule A image 202507159763270558, PDF page 74](https://docquery.fec.gov/pdf/485/202507159763270485/202507159763270485.pdf#page=74); FEC, [Earmarked contributions](https://www.fec.gov/updates/earmarked-contributions/); David-Seth Kirshner, [AIPAC: The Courage to Evolve](https://aipac.org/editorials/courage-to-evolve), editorial dated 21 October 2026, republished on AIPAC's website; Marc Rod, [AIPAC to end candidate endorsements, PAC contributions to allies](https://jewishinsider.com/2026/10/aipac-end-candidate-endorsements-pac-contributions/), *Jewish Insider*, 21 October 2026.

**Supports:** The July 2025 filing shows an individual contribution with AIPAC PAC identified in memo entries as the intermediary and states “TOTAL EARMARKED THROUGH CONDUIT. PAC LIMIT NOT AFFECTED.” The October editorial describes announced plans to end endorsements, direct PAC contributions, and public earmarking; the news report quotes the leadership letter describing those changes.

**Limits:** This is one filing example and does not establish the composition of all AIPAC PAC activity. The underlying individual and intermediary entries must not be double-counted as two separate contributions. The editorial is attributed commentary republished by AIPAC, not the original announcement; the quoted letter is available here through secondary reporting. These sources do not independently verify completed implementation, its exact timing, or PAC termination. The 2025 filing and the financial totals through 14 October 2026 predate the announcement.

### 18. United Democracy Project

**Source:** FEC, [United Democracy Project, C00799031](https://www.fec.gov/data/committee/C00799031/?cycle=2026&tab=spending).

**Supports:** The FEC identifies UDP as an active independent-expenditure-only super PAC. For 1 January 2025 through 14 October 2026, the page reported $112,820,861.12 in total receipts and $53,603,927.44 in independent expenditures.

**Limits:** Summary totals do not identify every ultimate human source or establish coordination or illegality. These sources do not document a particular core-AIPAC-to-UDP transfer or trace core AIPAC's undisclosed contributors into UDP. The guide's nonprofit-donor explanation describes a general disclosure pathway, not a verified transaction in this example. Individual transactions, amendments, and expenditure reports should be inspected for a particular claim.
