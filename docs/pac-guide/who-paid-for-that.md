# Who Paid for That? PACs, Super PACs, and the Missing Donor Layer

*A practical guide to reading political-money claims without confusing donations, contributions, transfers, lobbying, and independent spending.*

*Draft prepared 5 November 2026. Numbered markers link to separate [Source Notes](who-paid-for-that-notes.md), which identify the source, what it establishes, and its limits. This guide is educational, not legal advice.*

---

## One sentence can hide five different transactions

You see a post saying:

> “Candidate Rivera took $8 million from Citizens for Better Roads.”

That may sound like one organization deposited $8 million into Rivera’s campaign account. It could mean that. It could also mean something very different:

- individual donors gave limited contributions to Rivera through a PAC acting as a conduit;
- the PAC made its own limited contribution to Rivera;
- a super PAC spent $8 million on advertisements supporting Rivera without giving the campaign the money;
- a nonprofit transferred money to the super PAC, which then bought the advertisements;
- or the organization spent money lobbying officeholders rather than supporting Rivera’s election.

Those are not interchangeable. They involve different legal entities, limits, disclosure rules, and kinds of influence.

This guide does not ask you to assume that political spending is corrupt or harmless. It gives you a more useful starting point:

> **Which legal entity moved the money, what did it do, who received or benefited from it, and how far does the public record let us follow the source?**

## Start with the exact name and the exact verb

Political organizations often share a brand, officers, addresses, or policy goals while remaining legally distinct. A nonprofit, traditional PAC, and super PAC can all use similar names. A headline may call all three “the group.”

Write down the exact entity named in the filing or advertisement, including its Federal Election Commission identification number when one exists. Then replace vague verbs such as *backed*, *funded*, *gave*, or *received* with the action actually documented.

### Five verbs that should not be flattened

**Contributed.** Money or something of value was given to a candidate, party, or political committee and is governed by the rules applicable to contributions.

**Forwarded.** A conduit or intermediary passed along a contribution that an individual had directed to a candidate. The individual remains the contributor; the conduit does not become the source merely because its name appears in the transaction record. If the conduit directs or controls the choice of recipient, however, the contribution can count against both the donor's and the conduit's limits. That does not make it two separate payments.[[1]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n1)

**Transferred.** Money moved from one organization or account to another. A transfer identifies the immediate source. It may not reveal every person whose earlier donations supplied the transferring organization’s general treasury.

**Spent supporting or opposing.** An organization paid for advertisements or other communications benefiting or attacking a candidate. An independent expenditure is not money received by the candidate’s campaign. It is spending outside the campaign that expressly advocates election or defeat and is not coordinated with the candidate or party.[[2]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n2)

**Lobbied.** An organization sought to influence legislation or government policy. Lobbying, campaign contributions, and independent election spending can all create access or influence, but they are governed and disclosed through different systems.

Words have consequences here. “The campaign received $8 million” is false if a super PAC independently spent $8 million on advertising. “The candidate benefited from $8 million in outside spending” may be accurate.

## The main structures

The labels below describe federal campaign-finance structures. State rules can differ.

| Structure | What it generally does | Can it give directly to a federal candidate? | What the public normally sees |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Candidate committee** | Raises and spends money for a candidate’s campaign | It is the candidate’s authorized campaign | Periodic FEC reports of receipts, spending, debts, and itemized contributors under federal reporting rules |
| **Traditional PAC** | Raises limited contributions and may support candidates, parties, or other committees | Yes, within applicable limits | FEC registration and reports; itemized receipts and disbursements |
| **Connected PAC / separate segregated fund** | A corporation, labor organization, trade association, or membership organization establishes and administers the PAC | Yes, within applicable limits | The PAC’s reports and connected organization; the corporation or union cannot simply place unrestricted treasury money into the contribution account |
| **Nonconnected PAC** | A PAC not established by a connected organization; may solicit from the lawful general public | Yes, within applicable limits | FEC registration and reports |
| **Leadership PAC** | A nonconnected PAC associated with a federal officeholder or political leader, generally used to support other candidates | Yes, within applicable limits | Sponsor information and FEC reports; it is legally separate from the sponsor’s candidate committee[[3]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n3) |
| **Super PAC** | An independent-expenditure-only committee that raises unlimited funds for independent political activity | **No direct candidate contribution**; its spending must remain legally independent | Direct donors reported to the FEC, expenditures, vendors, and candidates supported or opposed[[4]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n4) |
| **Hybrid PAC** | Keeps a limited-contribution account and a separate unlimited non-contribution account | The contribution account may give within limits; the other account funds independent activity | Reports must separate the accounts and activity |
| **501(c)(4) social-welfare organization** | May lobby as a primary activity and may engage in some candidate-related political activity so long as that is not its primary activity | An **incorporated** 501(c)(4) cannot give treasury funds directly to a federal candidate; a separate eligible PAC may contribute within limits. Tax status alone does not determine corporate form or every election-law obligation | Public Form 990 information, but generally not the names and addresses of contributors; election-related reports may disclose particular spending or an immediate organizational donor[[5]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n5) |

“Dark money” is a common political and journalistic description, not one formal entity type. It usually refers to election-influencing money whose original donors are not publicly disclosed. The phrase can describe a genuine transparency gap. It does not, by itself, establish that the money was foreign, illegal, or given for a corrupt bargain.

## What the public trail can show

The FEC’s records are unusually detailed once you have the correct committee name or ID. A committee page can show its type, treasurer, connected organization, reporting schedule, receipts, contributions to other committees, independent expenditures, cash, debts, and original filings. Transaction records can identify dates, amounts, contributors, recipients, memo descriptions, and filing images.[[6]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n6)

For an advertisement, begin with the disclaimer. Independent communications generally must identify the person or organization that paid and say whether a candidate authorized them.[[7]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n7) That identifies the payor. It does not necessarily identify every person behind the payor.

For a nonprofit, look for:

- its exact legal name and Employer Identification Number;
- its tax classification;
- Form 990 revenue, spending, grants, related organizations, officers, and political or lobbying schedules;
- transfers to political committees;
- and whether the dates in the tax filing overlap the election activity you are examining.

A Form 990 is not a campaign-finance report and may cover a fiscal year rather than an election cycle. A large annual contribution total does not tell you that the whole amount was used in elections.

## Where the trail can stop

A super PAC reports its direct contributors. If a named person or business gives directly, the public record can usually identify that immediate source. But a nonprofit can give from its general treasury, and the super PAC’s report may identify only the nonprofit.

Most 501(c)(4) organizations are not required to reveal contributor names publicly. Under Treasury and IRS rules adopted in 2020, many tax-exempt organizations other than 501(c)(3) organizations and section 527 political organizations also no longer report substantial contributors’ names and addresses to the IRS annually on Schedule B, although they must keep records and provide them if the IRS properly requests them during an examination.[[8]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n8)

That creates an important distinction:

> **The public may know which organization transferred the money without knowing which human beings supplied that organization’s general treasury.**

This is a disclosure limit, not proof that no records exist. The recipient organization, banks, payment processors, accountants, and sometimes regulators may possess records the public cannot inspect.

It is also not automatically proper to assign every dollar in a nonprofit’s treasury to its largest known supporter. Money is fungible, but legal attribution still requires evidence about earmarking, restrictions, timing, accounts, or the organization’s own records.

## Why donor privacy exists

Public disclosure can help voters assess who is trying to influence an election. The Supreme Court has upheld important campaign disclaimer and disclosure requirements, reasoning that they inform voters without imposing a ceiling on political speech.[[9]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n9)

Associational privacy also protects real interests. In *NAACP v. Alabama*, compelled disclosure threatened members with reprisals and interfered with lawful association. In *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, the Court rejected California’s broad routine collection of major-donor information under the system at issue.[[10]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n10)

Those cases do not mean every political-money disclosure rule is unconstitutional. They show why the problem cannot be reduced to “honest donors have nothing to hide.” Disclosure can expose vulnerable people to harassment, employment consequences, or violence. Secrecy can also conceal concentrated influence, conflicts, or unlawful sources.

A serious policy asks both:

- What disclosure is sufficiently connected to election influence and enforcement?
- What protection is needed for people facing credible threats or reprisals?

## Foreign money, straw donors, and oversight

Federal law prohibits foreign nationals from directly or indirectly making contributions, donations, expenditures, or independent expenditures in connection with federal, state, or local elections. It also prohibits acting as a conduit for foreign-national money and restricts foreign participation in election-spending decisions. For individuals, this campaign-finance definition excludes U.S. citizens and people lawfully admitted for permanent residence (green-card holders). A surname or foreign address alone does not establish a prohibited source.[[11]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n11)

It is likewise unlawful to make a contribution in another person’s name. An organization cannot lawfully erase a prohibited donor by relabeling an American citizen as the source.[[12]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n12)

The oversight system is divided:

- **The FEC** has civil jurisdiction over federal campaign-finance law. It reviews reports, conducts audits, accepts sworn complaints, receives agency referrals, and can negotiate civil penalties.[[13]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n13)
- **The Justice Department** can prosecute knowing and willful criminal violations.
- **The IRS** administers tax-exempt status, returns, and tax rules governing nonprofit political activity.
- **State regulators** enforce separate state campaign-finance, charity, and election laws.

Legal authority and present enforcement capacity are different. The FEC needs at least four affirmative commissioner votes for official action and for each major enforcement stage to proceed. As of 5 November 2026, its published roster lists two commissioners and four vacancies, leaving it without a quorum for those decisions. That does not end reporting obligations, all staff functions, or the separate authority of the DOJ, IRS, and state regulators.

The FEC generally keeps enforcement matters confidential until they are closed, although complainants and respondents may disclose their own complaints or responses.[[14]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n14) A complaint can therefore be pending without public visibility, and a disclosure gap can make the evidence needed to trigger an investigation difficult to obtain.

“No public donor list” does not mean “no oversight.” It also does not mean the public can independently verify compliance donor by donor.

## Worked example: one brand, three different records

AIPAC provides a useful, dated example because three organizations that readers may casually call “AIPAC” appear in different records.

### Core AIPAC

The American Israel Public Affairs Committee is a 501(c)(4) lobbying and advocacy organization. Its Form 990 for the fiscal year ending September 2025 reported about $122.5 million in revenue, including about $113.4 million in contributions. The public filing does not identify the contributors behind that total.[[15]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n15)

That supports: “AIPAC’s core organization received substantial private contributions.”

It does **not** identify every donor, establish that the money was foreign, or show that every contributed dollar funded election activity.

### AIPAC PAC

The American Israel Public Affairs Committee Political Action Committee is FEC committee **C00797670**, a membership-organization PAC and lobbyist/registrant PAC. For the 2025–2026 period through October 14, 2026, the FEC summary reported about $57.8 million in receipts, mostly individual contributions, and about $53.3 million in contributions to other committees.[[16]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n16)

In the cited July 2025 candidate filing, AIPAC PAC acted as a conduit for an earmarked individual contribution. The filing identifies the individual donor and AIPAC PAC as the intermediary, with a memo explaining that the PAC limit was not affected.

This is historical activity, not a claim about an unchanged operating model. An editorial dated 21 October 2026 and republished on AIPAC's website described announced plans to stop PAC endorsements, direct candidate contributions, and public earmarking. Contemporaneous reporting quoted a leadership letter describing the same changes. Those sources establish an announcement; this guide has not independently verified when each change was implemented or that the PAC terminated.[[17]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n17)

That supports: “Individuals used AIPAC PAC to route disclosed, earmarked contributions to candidates,” when the relevant filings say so.

It does **not** support counting every conduit contribution as money donated from AIPAC PAC’s own treasury.

### United Democracy Project

United Democracy Project is FEC committee **C00799031**, an independent-expenditure-only super PAC. For the same cycle and cutoff, the FEC summary reported about $112.8 million raised and about $53.6 million in independent expenditures.[[18]](who-paid-for-that-notes.md#n18)

That spending may benefit or oppose candidates, but it is not money deposited in their campaign accounts. Direct donors to UDP appear in its FEC reports. As a general disclosure rule, when a nonprofit is a direct donor to a super PAC, the public report identifies that nonprofit; it may not reveal the humans who supplied its treasury. This guide does not document a particular core-AIPAC-to-UDP transfer. The summary figures above do not establish that pathway or trace money from core AIPAC's undisclosed contributors into UDP.

The point is not that one of these channels is automatically benign or corrupt. It is that a claim such as “Candidate X received AIPAC money” may combine:

1. AIPAC PAC’s own limited contribution.
2. Individual contributions forwarded through AIPAC PAC.
3. Independent spending by UDP.
4. Policy lobbying by core AIPAC.
5. A transfer between organizations, if a particular transaction is documented; its ultimate human sources may or may not be publicly visible.

A useful analysis separates them before adding numbers or assigning responsibility.

## Six questions to ask

### 1. Which exact entity?

Record the full legal name, committee ID or EIN, and entity type. Similar names are not proof that accounts are interchangeable.

### 2. What is the documented action?

Was it a direct contribution, conduit transaction, transfer, independent expenditure, lobbying expense, grant, or ordinary operating expense?

### 3. Who legally received the money?

Did money enter a candidate committee, another political committee, a vendor, or a nonprofit? A candidate can benefit without receiving the funds.

### 4. Whose identity is actually disclosed?

Distinguish the original individual contributor, intermediary, immediate organizational donor, payor, vendor, and ultimate beneficial source. Do not silently move from one layer to another.

### 5. What does the record not establish?

A filing can prove that a transaction was reported. It may not prove the source behind a general treasury, coordination, a quid pro quo, private motive, legal compliance, or the practical effect of the spending.

### 6. What oversight or remedy exists?

Identify the competent regulator, filing deadline, audit or complaint route, current procedural status, and actual outcome. A complaint is not a finding. An opaque trail is not an acquittal. A civil penalty may not repair the political effect of spending that already occurred.

## Closing: precision is part of accountability

Political money can shape who runs, which messages voters hear, what officeholders fear, and which policies receive sustained attention. Those effects deserve scrutiny.

Scrutiny becomes weaker, not stronger, when every kind of spending is called a donation to a candidate or every undisclosed donor is treated as proven foreign money. The inaccurate version is easier to dismiss.

Start with the entity. Use the right verb. Follow each transaction as far as the record permits. Mark where the trail ends, why it ends, and who has authority to look further.

**A calm question:** *“Which legal entity spent or transferred this money, who is identified as its immediate source, and does the record reveal the original human donor—or only another organization?”*
