## 2. Editorial purpose and boundaries

The reader has conflicting accounts, limited time, and a decision to make. Help them place more confidence where it is justified, reduce it where needed, or identify what remains unknown. Distrust is not the predetermined outcome; neither is reassurance or deference.

Explain the title early. Familiar sources may contradict each other; once-reassuring confidence may no longer help. This does not establish that all institutions have collapsed. The guide addresses conflicting information, not disaster survival, interpersonal abuse, clinical anxiety, or a uniquely bad historical era. A brief practical boundary should say not to delay immediate protective action to finish a source-checking exercise; do not add a second emergency lesson.

Give readers grounds for choosing people and organizations to rely on: relevant competence, access to the evidence, explanations that fit the record, and accountable handling of errors. Make trust specific to a question. This does not require ignoring a documented pattern of unreliability, nor does one mistake automatically discredit everything a person says.

Some mistrust can have sound reasons, including a person's experience of being misled or ignored. Do not diagnose skepticism as irrationality. Equally, an institution's past failure does not by itself establish the truth of every competing claim. A firsthand account can establish an experience without establishing its frequency; an average does not erase a documented exception.

Keep distinctions practical:

- A record of what someone said is evidence of the statement, not proof that its contents are true.
- Relevant expertise matters; prestige in an unrelated field is insufficient. A capable synthesis may be more useful than an uninterpretable raw paper.
- Official status, outsider status, sincerity, confidence, popularity, and political agreement do not settle a claim.
- Factual findings, forecasts, explanations, and choices about priorities can overlap, but they answer different questions.
- Disclosure, uncertainty, and corrections supply information to assess. None is an automatic certificate of reliability.
- Scrutiny should use the same standards for comforting and alarming claims, allies and opponents. Equal standards do not imply equal weight or equal blame.

Do not turn this into a catalogue of cognitive biases, media-brand rankings, a misinformation encyclopedia, or instructions for becoming an amateur specialist. Avoid academic labels where ordinary words work. Use a warm, direct voice without treating the reader as gullible. These editorial guardrails do not each require a separate passage or caveat: use them to test wording, and keep only decisive limitations in the main text. The required teaching content is the five-question method, two exercises, and a feasible next step.

## 3. Reader method and suggested structure

These five questions are our teaching aid. The exact combination has not been tested. They are not a score, diagnostic, or guarantee, and not every low-stakes claim needs all five.

### 1. What exactly differs?

Put the competing assertions into short sentences. Check date, place, population, definitions, and outcome. A changed situation is different from a corrected error. Ask whether the disagreement concerns what happened, what caused it, what might happen next, or what should matter. Some disagreements survive these checks; do not promise that every conflict dissolves with better wording.

### 2. How could each source know?

Look for the basis of the decisive assertion: direct observation, a relevant record, suitable expertise, a study, another report, or inference. Ask whether the source's work or experience fits this question. Show how a knowledgeable outsider can provide a better record than an official account, without implying that outsider status itself confers accuracy. Do not require a third full scenario to make this point.

Explain in ordinary language the usefulness of briefly leaving an unfamiliar page to learn who produced it and how others have assessed its claims. Searching is a route to evidence, not evidence by itself. A search result's position and several agreeing snippets do not establish truth. For specialized disputes, look for a competent synthesis that identifies its evidence and important limits; readers need not reproduce the analysis themselves.

### 3. Are these separate reasons, or repeated versions of one?

Follow the decisive assertion far enough to find its supporting source. Several stories copying one announcement add no independent observation. Independent reporting can add interviews, records, or checks; independent analysis of shared data can also be informative. Do not treat all shared evidence as defective or count URLs as votes.

Include at most one short sentence applying the same rule to AI: agreement between AI answers does not establish independent confirmation; inspect the sources supporting the particular claim. This is a provenance rule, not a quantified claim about all models or a claim that every AI answer is wrong.

### 4. Which account fits the evidence better, including its limits?

Consider relevance, quality of the underlying work, scope, contrary evidence, and what remains uncertain. Relevant expert convergence can justify reliance without unanimity. Explain that readers can use a current, accountable synthesis that describes how agreement was assessed; they need not count experts themselves. A contrary opinion deserves assessment of its evidence, not automatic equal weight. A genuine dispute may remain unresolved.

For conflicts, ask who funded or benefits from the work and what influence they had over the evidence or its release. Apply that question to commercial, governmental, nonprofit, and personal interests without asserting that their risks are identical. Disclosure alone does not remove a conflict, and a conflict alone does not prove falsity.

For corrections, ask what changed, why, and whether the conclusion survives. Distinguish a changed situation, an inference revised with new evidence, and an earlier mistaken report, while allowing overlap. Whether an error was preventable needs separate evidence. Repeated unsupported claims after correction may warrant less confidence. Do not infer intent without evidence, or claim that admitting an error proves honesty in general.

### 5. What would change my answer, and what do I need to do now?

Name one useful missing check rather than demanding endless research. Allow conclusions such as “supported for this narrow question,” “plausible but unresolved,” or “not established by the evidence offered.” Match the effort to the stakes, urgency, and reversibility of the decision. Sometimes an appropriate qualified person is needed; sometimes a dated operational notice resolves the issue.

Uncertainty does not always justify postponing action, and inability to obtain evidence is not proof that an event did not happen. Acknowledge constraints on time, money, access, language, and safety. Give a stopping rule: stop when a proportionate check answers the actual question well enough for the decision; if it does not, state the gap and choose a feasible next step. Avoid promising a universal number of sources or minutes.

### Word budget and research placement

Aim for about 1,800 words: 180 for the opening and scope, 900 for the method, 420 for two exercises and explanations, and 200 for limits and a practical close. These are planning amounts, not rigid quotas. Integrate related limits instead of repeating them in every section.

Use at most two brief research passages in the guide; detailed designs and samples belong in notes. One should explain why leaving a page to investigate can help but more searching is not automatically better. Preserve that limitation in the main prose. Do not make the main text a tour of seven publications, and do not cite a source simply because it appears in the ledger.

## 4. Two fictional exercises

Clearly label both as invented teaching examples, including every date and number. Offer a question before the explanation. These illustrate reasoning; they are not evidence that the method works.

### Exercise A: Is the library closed on Saturdays?

On a Friday, a neighborhood post says the library is now closed on Saturdays. Its screenshot shows a library notice suspending Saturday opening. A local newsletter says normal Saturday hours have resumed. Opening the linked notices reveals that the suspension applied to a building repair in September; a current dated notice says the same branch resumed Saturday hours on October 3. State that the fictional reader is checking after that date.

Ask which claim is supported now and what, if anything, still needs checking before a visit. The current direct operational notice supports resumed hours more strongly than the old screenshot. It does not guarantee against a new closure, settle unrelated disputes about library management, or prove deliberate deception by the poster. Keep the caveat proportionate: do not train readers to remain suspicious after an ordinary question has been adequately answered.

### Exercise B: Did the market double attendance?

Three websites say a weekend market doubled attendance. All cite one organizer's release. Its table shows Saturday attendance rose from 100 to 200. A critic cites that same table: the old two-day weekend drew 300 attendees; the new Saturday-only event drew 200. For this fictional exercise, specify comparable counts, the same coverage, and no duplicate counting within a weekend.

Ask whether there are three independent confirmations, which comparison each statement uses, and whether either figure establishes success. Saturday attendance doubled; whole-weekend attendance fell by one third. Both scoped comparisons fit the stated record, but unqualified “attendance doubled” omits a consequential distinction. Several reports repeat one evidentiary chain; the critic's interpretation is not a second independent measurement either.

Success depends on the question and priorities: a busier Saturday, total participation, access on Sunday, or something else. Whether the new schedule caused the change remains unestablished; the table alone supplies no comparison that isolates the schedule's effect. This unresolved causal question should stay visible. Do not invent revenue, cost, corruption, or wellbeing effects. The organizer's own record can help expose the weakness of an expansive promotional claim.

## 5. Evidence ledger

This is a targeted research packet, not a systematic review. Entries distinguish experiments, descriptive research, committee guidance, and our editorial adaptations. Inspection means the identified text was retrieved on 9 October 2026; it does not mean the underlying data were reanalyzed. Access gaps are not evidence of a defect in a paper.

### S01 — What professional fact-checkers did

**Wineburg, S., & McGrew, S. (2019).** “Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information.” *Teachers College Record, 121*(11), 1–40. [DOI and publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102).

**Inspected:** Publisher's author-written abstract; full-text retrieval failed.

**Evidence:** A think-aloud comparison involved 10 professional fact-checkers, 10 PhD historians, and 25 Stanford undergraduates. Across three tasks involving live websites, fact-checkers investigated outside the initial site and reached better-warranted judgments more efficiently.

**Limits:** A small descriptive comparison of selected groups, not a randomized intervention or a population estimate. It identifies a useful practice without proving every fact-checker correct or showing that this short guide reproduces their expertise. Avoid effect sizes, detailed scoring claims, or assertions about all historians or students without full-text inspection.

### S02 — Teaching students to investigate outside a page

**Wineburg, S., Breakstone, J., McGrew, S., Smith, M. D., & Ortega, T. (2022).** “Lateral Reading on the Open Internet: A District-Wide Field Study in High School Government Classes.” *Journal of Educational Psychology, 114*(5), 893–909. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000740); [author-project summary and paper link](https://cor.inquirygroup.org/research/lateral-reading-on-the-open-internet/); [ERIC abstract record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1372738).

**Inspected:** Author-project summary, final-article abstract record, and earlier SSRN abstract; full paper not retrieved.

**Evidence:** A cluster-randomized study in one urban school district compared instruction with control conditions and reported improved assessment of online credibility. The final abstract describes six 50-minute lessons; this was substantially more instruction than a short article.

**Limits:** Students, a particular curriculum, and assessment tasks do not establish lasting changes in adults' everyday decisions. Assignment details and effect estimates need full-text inspection before further claims. The earlier SSRN abstract says six one-hour lessons; prefer the final abstract, or omit duration. S01 and S02 share investigators and are not wholly independent research programmes.

### S03 — Searching can also reinforce a false claim

**Aslett, K., Sanderson, Z., Godel, W., Persily, N., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. A. (2024).** “Online searches to evaluate misinformation can increase its perceived veracity.” *Nature, 625*, 548–556. [DOI and publisher](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06883-y); [author-institution PDF](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/s41586-023-06883-y.pdf). Published online December 20, 2023; issue January 18, 2024.

**Inspected:** PDF pp. 548–551: abstract, designs and reported results; later retrieval was blocked. Publisher redirected to an inaccessible page; PMC required a browser check.

**Evidence:** Five US experiments found increased belief in selected false or misleading news following search prompts. Designs included randomized between-group and within-person before/after comparisons. Browser records in study 5 associated the effect with lower-quality search results.

**Limits:** This is not evidence that every search misleads or that trained source investigation is ineffective. The prompt effect and the proposed search-quality mechanism have different causal support; returned-result quality was not randomly assigned. Article truth ratings used fact-checker judgments; study 5 assessed search-result source quality using NewsGuard ratings. Do not translate results into a universal percentage. Useful boundary: search is a route to evidence, not confirmation in itself.

### S04 — Brief search instruction has limited and variable effects

**Oswald, L., Kozyreva, A., Herzog, S. M., Nickl, P. L., & Hertwig, R. (2026).** “Boosting Media Literacy Using Lateral Reading and Online Search Interventions.” *Psychological Science, 37*(8), 527–539. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261453813); [official society abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976261453813/).

**Inspected:** Official abstract and publication metadata only; no full-text method or supplement claim is cleared here.

**Evidence:** The authors describe a nationally representative randomized study of 2,666 German adults comparing source-focused and claim-focused interventions. Improvements were small, and not all were statistically distinguishable from zero. At two weeks, treatment/control differences were no longer statistically distinguishable. The abstract reports no evidence of backfire.

**Limits:** Nonsignificance does not establish zero effect or prove equivalence. Sampling details, outcome construction, attrition, and estimates need full-text inspection before further claims. This recent result prevents an unqualified promise of durable benefits; it does not contradict every earlier finding. Suitable principally for notes, with access limits stated.

### S05 — Weigh the body of evidence

**National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2019).** *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science*, chapter 7, “Confidence in Science,” pp. 143–162. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17226/25303); [official chapter](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303/chapter/10).

**Inspected:** Synthesis discussion, pp. 143–145, and conclusions/recommendations, pp. 160–161, particularly Recommendation 7-3.

**Guidance:** Assess a finding against the wider relevant evidence before consequential decisions. Research synthesis examines methods and relevance rather than treating study counts as votes. One contrary result does not automatically overturn multiple supporting lines of evidence.

**Limits:** Committee guidance and synthesis, not an experiment validating a reader method. This does not mean a single decisive study can never overturn a conclusion, or that every review is sound. Use for the broader-evidence question, not a digression into replication controversies.

### S06 — Examine conflicts and the substance of corrections

**International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (January 2026 update).** *Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing, and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals*, sections II.B, pp. 3–4, and III.A, p. 8. [Official PDF](https://www.icmje.org/icmje-recommendations.pdf); [disclosure section](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/author-responsibilities--conflicts-of-interest.html).

**Inspected:** Dated PDF, both sections, and disclosure HTML.

**Guidance:** Relationships can create conflicts without necessarily distorting a paper. Disclosure should identify funding and sponsor roles, including control over analysis and publication. Corrections should specify changes and versions; errors that invalidate conclusions may require retraction. Evolving knowledge and ordinary debate are not themselves errors.

**Limits:** Medical publishing standards, not proof of compliance or a general credibility test. Extending these questions to civic sources is our adaptation. Disclosure does not eliminate a conflict; a correction neither proves general reliability nor establishes deliberate dishonesty.

### S07 — Relevant expertise, substantive dissent, and local knowledge

**National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2026).** “Earning and Maintaining Trust,” in *On Being a Scientist*, fourth edition, version 1.0, updated September 2026. [Version DOI](https://doi.org/10.17226/29488/3.1.0); [versioned official text](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/pubs/29488/versions/v1.0/modules/module-3.html).

**Inspected:** Researcher trustworthiness, science communication, and community-engagement sections; version date.

**Guidance:** Acknowledge limits of expertise, uncertainty and substantive dissent; distinguish evidence from policy advocacy; explain the basis of consensus; engage with responses to criticism. Communities may possess relevant knowledge researchers lack.

**Limits:** Ethical guidance informed by scholarship, not experimental validation of our questions. Its discussion of perceived trust does not establish factual accuracy. Credentials, humility, consensus, dissent, and lived experience each require attention to the specific question. The guide does not need this module's historical cases, which were not independently checked here.

### How the sources constrain the draft

Do not portray these studies as interchangeable: expert observation, substantial classroom instruction, a brief intervention, and an encouragement to search examine different things. They do not jointly validate this five-question guide. Perceived truth, task accuracy, source trust, and actual decision quality are different outcomes.

The two National Academies publications are related institutional guidance, not two independent experiments. ICMJE addresses medical publishing; our use of comparable questions for civic information is an explicit editorial adaptation. The exercises, stopping rule, same-standard principle, and distinction between repeated reports and new observations are reasoning tools, not experimentally established effects of this article.

Read the original source for any factual claim you use where access permits. If you cannot, identify that gap in your report, narrow the claim, or omit it. Do not silently describe an abstract as full-text inspection. Check for corrections or newer versions of sources actually cited. Do not add unverified statistics, inferred motives, dramatic anecdotes, or unsupported claims about how “people today” behave.

## 6. Adversarial review checklist

Before handing the draft back, assess these points with concrete passages. A self-review is not an independent review.

1. **Title:** Does the opening define the felt breakdown without declaring universal collapse or promising emergency expertise?
2. **Usefulness:** Does the guide help choose whom to rely on for a particular question, rather than merely multiply doubts? Can a reader reach warranted confidence and stop?
3. **Transfer:** Would the questions work unchanged on the reader's favorite commentator, an official source, a dissenter, a seller, and this guide itself?
4. **Authority:** Is relevant competence given weight without treating prestige, affiliation, sincerity, or outsider status as proof? Is a competent synthesis an acceptable endpoint?
5. **Disagreement:** Are different dates and definitions, genuine factual disputes, uncertainty, and values distinguished? Does at least one consequential question remain unresolved where the evidence cannot settle it?
6. **Independence:** Are independent observations, independent analysis, and copies of one report distinguished? Have AI agreement and search ranking been denied automatic evidentiary weight without blanket claims of unreliability?
7. **Weight:** Does relevant expert agreement matter without hiding substantive dissent or giving every objection equal standing? Are standards equal without manufactured partisan balance?
8. **Corrections and conflicts:** Does the reader learn what to examine, rather than get simplistic rules such as “funded means false” or “corrects means trustworthy”?
9. **Evidence:** Are designs, outcomes, populations, and causal claims accurate? Are empirical findings distinguished from professional guidance and editorial reasoning? Is the method explicitly unvalidated?
10. **Burden and safety:** Can someone with limited time or access take a feasible next step? Does the method avoid delaying urgent action or demanding specialist judgment from every reader?
11. **Exercises:** Are they unmistakably fictional? Do the numbers and dates cohere? Do we avoid claims about intent, causation, future outcomes, or success that the supplied facts cannot establish?
12. **Reader perspectives:** Identify concrete wording that could reasonably trouble a conservative, progressive, disengaged reader, someone with grounds for institutional mistrust, or someone without easy access to expert help. Do not invent their beliefs or require equal-sized political criticisms.
13. **Deliverables:** Is the guide within 1,500–2,000 words? Do every citation and return link resolve using the actual filenames? Are source access gaps, substantive departures, and untested assumptions reported honestly?

Finish with the three deliverables requested in section 1, ready for an independent factual and editorial review of actual prose.
