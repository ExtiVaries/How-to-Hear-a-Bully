## Confirmed defects, access gaps and editorial judgments

That matches the author-project summary, and the Stanford news release says "randomized experiment." But the final abstract on ERIC says "a matched control design" and does not mention randomization. I could not resolve this without the full paper. **Smallest correction:** the guide says classes given six lessons "improved ... compared with classes taught as usual" and never says "randomized"; the note records the conflict. ### Substantive departures from the brief (editorial judgments)

1. **Note 4 is cited in the main text.** The packet marked Oswald et al. "principally for notes." I kept one sentence because it is the decisive limit on promising that brief instruction helps, and this guide *is* brief instruction. A reviewer could reasonably move it to the notes and save ~25 words.
2. **Note 4 wording is narrower than the packet.** The packet says differences "were no longer statistically distinguishable" at two weeks, which is accurate. The abstract adds that "discernment improved in all groups," including controls. So the guide says the trained groups were "no longer measurably ahead," not that the effect "faded."
3. **One research passage, but a dense one.** The packet allows at most two brief research passages. All four studies sit in one paragraph of about 150 words under question 2. Notes 5–7 are cited as guidance in question 4, not described as research. A reviewer may judge that paragraph too long for "brief."
4. **Safety callout at the top, not in the method.** One line, modeled on the crisis guide. There is no second emergency passage.
5. **Exercise B wording.** I added "after the market became a Saturday-only event" so the 100→200 Saturday figure and the 300→200 weekend figure read as the same change. Numbers unchanged: 100 to 200 is a doubling; 300 to 200 is a fall of one third; old Sunday attendance is implied at 200, which the guide does not state.
6. **Exercise A gives a stopping point.** It ends "the question is answered, and you can go," with one optional check for a long trip. This is my reading of the brief's instruction not to train lingering suspicion.
7. **New illustrations not in the packet:** the mechanic/tax-return line and the resident with dated photos of a streetlight (the brief's "knowledgeable outsider" point). Both are invented and unmarked as such because they are ordinary illustrations, not scenarios with data.

## Self-review against the section 6 checklist

1. **Title.** The opening defines the breakdown as "a loss of bearings," says it "is not a claim that every institution has failed," and scopes out disasters, mistreatment and anxiety. Passes, I think. The callout's "whoever is responsible for safety where you are" is vague by design.
2. **Usefulness.** The stopping rule, partial verdicts and Exercise A's "you can go" let a reader reach confidence and stop. The weakest point is question 4, which is long and could read as a list of further doubts.
3. **Transfer.** The questions avoid naming source types, and "Limits" asks readers to apply them to this guide. "Agreeing with your politics" is listed among non-grounds without suggesting which politics.
4. **Authority.** "Expertise in one field doesn't carry over"; outsider status and official status are both listed as non-grounds; the streetlight example credits the record, not outsider status. A competent synthesis is explicitly an acceptable endpoint (questions 2 and 4).
5. **Disagreement.** Question 1 separates dates/definitions from what happened, cause, forecast and priorities. Exercise B leaves causation and "success" unresolved.
6. **Independence.** Question 3 distinguishes copies, independent reporting and independent analysis of shared data; one sentence on AI answers; search ranking denied weight in question 2. No blanket claim that search or AI is unreliable.
7. **Weight.** Expert agreement can justify reliance "without waiting for unanimity"; dissent gets "a look at its evidence, not automatic equal time"; visible-debate guidance cited. "Equal standards don't mean equal weight or equal blame."
8. **Corrections and conflicts.** Both get "what to examine" questions; explicitly rejects "conflict proves false" and "correction proves honest."
9. **Evidence.** Note 2's design is the open problem (see above). The guide calls note 1 "a small study of selected people" and says no cited research tested the five questions. The notes label each source's kind and distinguish research from guidance and adaptation.
10. **Burden and safety.** Callout puts action first; question 5 names time, money, language, access and safety; the next step needs no tools. "Sometimes you need a qualified person" offers no route for readers who can't reach one, which is a gap.
11. **Exercises.** Labeled invented, with every place, date and number fictional. Arithmetic checked. No intent, causation or success is inferred.
12. **Reader perspectives** (wording that could reasonably trouble someone):
    - *Someone with grounds for institutional mistrust:* "A dissent deserves a look at its evidence, not automatic equal time" and "One new contrary study shouldn't, by itself, be taken to overturn conclusions" could read as siding with established views. "Some distrust is earned" is meant to balance it.
    - *Conservative reader:* both cited guidance bodies are establishment institutions (National Academies, medical editors), and "Medical journal standards" may read as appeal to authority. "Campaigns" in the interests list is neutral but could be read as aimed at activists.
    - *Progressive reader:* "companies" sits in a list with "charities" and "campaigns" "without assuming their risks are identical"; someone may read that as false equivalence, or as too soft on commercial funding.
    - *Disengaged reader:* ~1,900 words and five questions is a lot. The "next step" asks only for question 1, which helps.
    - *Someone without access to expert help:* see item 10.
13. **Deliverables.** Within range; links verified by script; gaps and departures reported above.
