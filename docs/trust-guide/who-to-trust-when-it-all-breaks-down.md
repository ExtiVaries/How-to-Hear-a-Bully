# Who to Trust When It All Breaks Down?

*A practical guide to conflicting claims, expertise, and uncertainty.*

*Numbered markers link to a separate file, [Source Notes](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md), which lists each source and its limits. You don't need the notes to follow the guide.*

> **If someone is in immediate danger,** take appropriate protective action and seek emergency help. Don't delay urgent action to work through these questions.

---

## When familiar sources stop agreeing

Two outlets you usually rely on describe the same event in incompatible ways. An official notice says one thing; a neighbor who was there says another. The voices that used to settle questions for you have become part of the argument, and the confidence that once helped you decide no longer does.

That is the breakdown in this guide's title: a loss of bearings. It is not a claim that every institution has failed, and it doesn't assume anyone is lying. People who are all sincere can still disagree, because they saw different things, counted differently, or care about different outcomes.

This guide is about choosing whom to rely on, for what, when accounts conflict. It is not about surviving a disaster, dealing with someone who mistreats you, or managing anxiety. Its aim is not to make you more distrustful, or less. It is to help you put more confidence where the evidence supports it, pull it back where it doesn't, and name what nobody yet knows.

## Trust is for a question

You don't have to trust anyone about everything. A mechanic may be the best judge of your brakes and no help with your tax return. Trust works better when it's attached to a question: who is in a position to know *this*?

Good grounds for relying on someone include relevant competence, access to the evidence, an explanation that fits the record, and a way of handling mistakes that you can see. Several things feel like grounds but don't settle a claim on their own: official status, outsider status, sincerity, confidence, popularity, and agreeing with your politics.

Some distrust is earned. If you have been misled or ignored before, caution is a reasonable response, not a flaw. A documented pattern of unreliability counts. But one mistake doesn't discredit everything a person says, and an institution's past failure doesn't make every competing claim true.

## Five questions

These questions are our own teaching aid. They haven't been tested with readers, they aren't a score, and a small, low-stakes question rarely needs all five.

### 1. What exactly differs?

Write each competing claim as a short sentence. Then check the date, the place, who is included, how terms are defined, and what outcome is measured. Some conflicts shrink here: one account is older, or counts something different. A situation that has changed is not the same as an error that was corrected.

Then ask what kind of disagreement is left. Is it about what happened, what caused it, what will happen next, or what should matter most? Evidence can often settle the first. The last is partly about priorities. Some disagreements survive careful wording, and that is worth knowing too.

### 2. How could each source know?

Find the basis of the claim that decides the matter. Did they see it, hold a relevant record, have suitable expertise, run a study, repeat another report, or infer it? Ask whether their work or experience fits this question; expertise in one field doesn't automatically carry over to another. A resident who kept dated photos of a broken streetlight may have a better record of it than an official summary written elsewhere. That is because of the photos, not because the resident is an outsider.

When a page is unfamiliar, it often helps to leave it briefly and look up who produced it and what others say about its claims. Researchers who watched professional fact-checkers saw them do this: they left unfamiliar sites quickly and reached better-supported judgments, in less time, than historians and students who stayed on the page.[[1]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n1)<a id="r1"></a> That was a small study of selected people. In one school district, classes given six lessons in the habit improved at judging online sources compared with classes taught as usual.[[2]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n2)<a id="r2"></a>

But more searching isn't automatically better. In a set of experiments, asking people to search online about false news stories made them somewhat more likely to believe them, especially when their results were low quality.[[3]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n3)<a id="r3"></a> And a recent trial of brief training for adults found small improvements; two weeks later, their differences from an untrained comparison group were no longer statistically clear.[[4]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n4)<a id="r4"></a>

So treat a search as a route to evidence, not as evidence. A top result, or several snippets that agree, doesn't make a claim true. For specialized disputes you don't need to redo the analysis. Look for a competent summary that says what it rests on and what it can't show.

### 3. Are these separate reasons, or one reason repeated?

Follow the claim back far enough to find where it came from. Five stories copying one announcement give you one source, not five. Independent reporting can add something new: interviews, records, or checks. Independent analysis of the same data can also tell you something, so shared evidence isn't a defect. The point is not to count links as votes. The same goes for AI tools: if several AI answers agree, look at the sources behind the particular claim rather than treating the agreement as confirmation.

### 4. Which account fits the evidence better, including its limits?

Weigh relevance, the quality of the underlying work, its scope, the evidence against it, and what is still uncertain. When people with relevant expertise broadly agree, that can justify relying on them without waiting for unanimity. You don't have to tally experts yourself: a current review that names its authors and evidence and explains how it judged agreement can do that work. One new contrary study shouldn't, by itself, be taken to overturn conclusions resting on several lines of evidence.[[5]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n5)<a id="r5"></a> A dissent deserves a look at its evidence, not automatic equal time. Guidance written for scientists asks them to make substantive debates visible, admit the limits of their expertise, and make clear when they are assessing evidence and when they are advocating a policy. It also recognizes that communities can know things researchers don't.[[6]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n6)<a id="r6"></a> Sometimes a real dispute stays unresolved, and the honest answer is to say so.

Two more things are worth examining.

**Interests.** Who funded or benefits from the work, and what control did they have over the evidence or its release? Ask this of companies, governments, charities, campaigns and individuals alike, without assuming their risks are identical. Medical journal standards ask authors to disclose what role funders had in design, analysis and the decision to publish, and ask journals to spell out what a correction changed.[[7]](who-to-trust-when-it-all-breaks-down-notes.md#n7)<a id="r7"></a> Disclosure tells you where to look; it doesn't remove a conflict. And a conflict doesn't prove a claim false.

**Corrections.** What changed, why, and does the conclusion still stand? A changed situation, a conclusion revised as new evidence arrived, and an earlier report that was simply wrong are different things, though they can overlap. Whether a mistake could have been avoided needs its own evidence. A correction doesn't prove someone honest in general. Repeating a claim after it has been shown to be unsupported is a reason for less confidence.

Use the same standards on comforting claims and alarming ones, on allies and opponents. Equal standards don't mean equal weight or equal blame.

### 5. What would change my answer, and what do I do now?

Name one missing check that would actually help. Then match your effort to how much is at stake, how urgent it is, and whether the decision can be undone. Sometimes you need a qualified person. Sometimes a current, dated notice settles it.

Answers can be partial: "supported for this narrow question," "plausible but unresolved," or "not shown by the evidence offered." Uncertainty doesn't always justify waiting, and failing to find evidence isn't proof that something didn't happen. Time, money, language, access and safety all limit how much checking is possible.

**When to stop:** stop when a check that fits the decision answers the actual question well enough. If it doesn't, say what's missing and choose a next step you can actually take.

## Worked example: when a pollster calls fabrication an experiment {#wedgewood-polls-2026-10-20}

*Real case: October 20–21, 2026 · Sources checked: October 21, 2026*

On October 20, Wedgewood Polls acknowledged fabricating data behind most of its releases. It described an experiment to expose polling-industry weaknesses and claimed one genuine Nebraska poll as an exception. Those are three separate claims: an admission about the data, an explanation of purpose, and an asserted exception.

**What exactly differs?** The admission is evidence about what Wedgewood says it did. It does not independently establish the purpose or the Nebraska exception.

**How could each source know?** FiftyPlusOne reports investigating the data and requesting comment before the admission. Its account supports its reported actions; this guide has not independently audited the survey files.

**Are these separate reasons?** Several outlets repeating the same poll do not supply several independent confirmations. Ask what records or checks each added. This does not establish that every publisher knowingly repeated falsehoods.

**Which account fits, including its limits?** “Would they have told us if nobody caught them?” is a fair question, but timing cannot answer it. A private plan could have existed. A secondary report says Wedgewood later claimed it planned three more polls and a reveal after the midterms. The original reply, its timestamp and any advance record remain unverified. The report does not independently establish an advance plan or erase admitted fabrication. [Sources](#wedgewood-sources)

**What would change the answer, and what now?** Authenticated earlier records could support the explanation. Ask what was supposed to end the project and how people who relied on the polls would be told. Silver Bulletin announced Wedgewood’s permanent exclusion from its averages and forecasts; an announced response does not establish that every affected output was corrected or every reader reached.

You can reject admitted fabricated results while leaving motive unresolved. Purpose, justification and repair are separate questions. Apply the same standard when the claimed lesson supports your own views.

## Practice

Both exercises are invented. Every place, date and number in them is fictional. They show reasoning; they are not evidence that the method works.

### Exercise A: Is the library closed on Saturdays?

On a Friday, a neighborhood post says the local library is now closed on Saturdays. It includes a screenshot of a library notice suspending Saturday opening. A local newsletter says normal Saturday hours are back. You open the notices themselves. The one in the screenshot applied to a building repair in September. A current, dated notice from the same branch says Saturday hours resumed on October 3. You are checking after that date.

*Which claim is supported now? Does anything still need checking before you go?*

The library's own current notice is the stronger evidence. It comes from the people who set the hours, and it is newer. The old notice described the September closure; it does not establish the hours now. Nothing here shows the poster meant to mislead. A new closure is always possible, so if the trip is long, a glance at the library's page that morning is reasonable. Otherwise the question is answered, and you can go.

### Exercise B: Did the market double attendance?

Three websites say a weekend market "doubled attendance." All three cite one release from the organizer. Its table shows that after the market became a Saturday-only event, Saturday attendance rose from 100 to 200. A critic cites the same table: the old two-day weekend drew 300 people; the new Saturday-only market drew 200. Assume all counts were made the same way, covered the same area, and counted no one twice within a weekend.

*Are there three independent confirmations? What comparison does each claim use? Does either figure show the change worked?*

There is one evidentiary source here, not four. The websites repeat the release, and the critic reinterprets the same table rather than counting again. Both comparisons fit the record: Saturday attendance doubled, and total weekend attendance fell by a third. "Attendance doubled," with no qualifier, leaves out the second fact, and it is the organizer's own table that shows it.

Whether the change was a success depends on what you care about: a busier Saturday, more people overall, or access for people who could only come on Sunday. That is a question of priorities, not arithmetic. And the table can't tell us whether the new schedule caused either change, because nothing in it separates the schedule's effect from anything else that differed. That question stays open.

## Limits, and a next step

This guide can't tell you who is right in any particular dispute, and it can't replace someone with the expertise a serious decision needs. The research cited here studied particular groups doing particular tasks. None of it tested these five questions, and they haven't been tried with readers. Apply them to this guide too: ask how we could know, and check the notes.

A next step you can take today: the next time two sources you rely on disagree about something that matters to you, write the two claims as short sentences and ask the first question. That may be enough. If it isn't, ask how each source could know, and stop when the answer is good enough for the decision in front of you.
