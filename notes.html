Verification Appendix

Each case below lists the original source, the relevant context, any contrary evidence, why it was classified the way it was, and what remains uncertain.

McCarthy (1950)

  • Original sources: no recording of the Wheeling speech exists. The Wheeling Intelligencer reported "205," but its reporter worked from the prepared text (e-WV, note 11). The State Department memo (note 11) records the 81 names, really 80, that McCarthy gave the Senate committee.
  • Context: 42 of the 80 worked at the department on Feb. 9, 1950. Earlier congressional reviews had named none of them as disloyal. The Senate censured McCarthy on Dec. 2, 1954, for his conduct toward the Senate (National Archives).
  • Contrary evidence: Soviet espionage in the U.S. government in this era was real, as later documented by historians. McCarthy's specific charges are a separate question.
  • Classification: unsubstantiated accusations that shifted the burden to the accused. He supplied names but did not substantiate his advertised accusations.
  • Uncertain: his exact wording and number at Wheeling.

Reid and Romney (2012–2015)

  • Original sources: the July 31, 2012 interview and Aug. 2 floor remarks (note 12), and the 2015 CNN transcript (note 13), which includes "Let him prove that he has paid taxes, because he hasn't" and "Romney didn't win, did he?"
  • Context: Romney released his 2010 and 2011 returns, plus a PwC letter reporting a lowest annual effective federal rate of 13.66% for 1990–2009 (note 14). A positive rate in every year implies he paid tax every year. The letter is a summary from his tax preparer, not the underlying returns. In 2015 Reid told Fusion, "Of course he paid taxes — what he didn't do is let us see his tax returns," and that he had no regrets (Washington Times).
  • Contrary evidence: asking for more tax returns was a legitimate and widely shared demand.
  • Classification: an unsupported accusation that shifted the burden of proof. It was not labeled "motte-and-bailey," because that would require evidence of a deliberate two-step strategy, and the record doesn't show one.
  • Uncertain: whether Reid believed his source. The "Let him prove…" line dates to Aug. 2, 2012 (note 13); CNN replayed it in 2015.

Trump on ISIS (2016)

  • Original sources: the Hewitt transcript (note 15), and the tweet and rally remarks reported by Roll Call (note 16).
  • Context: in the same interview Trump both rejected Hewitt's "vacuum" reading and argued that Obama's Iraq policy caused ISIS. He also said of the word "founder": "Everyone's liking it."
  • Contrary evidence: the policy-causation argument is a real position that many people hold. ISIS grew out of al-Qaeda in Iraq, which existed before Obama took office.
  • Classification: shifting among strong wording, a policy claim and "sarcasm." His private intent is not determined.
  • Uncertain: which reading his audiences actually took.

Biden on Facebook (2021), a boundary case

  • Original sources: the PBS video and report from July 16 (note 17) and the July 19 transcript (note 18).
  • Context: the original question was already about vaccine misinformation on platforms. The report Biden cited, from the Center for Countering Digital Hate, named 12 individuals (CCDH). Facebook disputed its methods (Meta). Neither side established how many deaths, if any, could be attributed to the platforms.
  • Classification: a clarification. There is no documented return to the stronger claim, so it is not classified as manipulation.
  • Uncertain: whether the original phrase was loose wording or deliberate.

2020 election claims

  • Original sources: Barr's Dec. 1, 2020 AP interview (note 22), the House Jan. 6 committee report (GovInfo, ch. 4), and the AP review of Dec. 14, 2021 (note 23).
  • Context: the AP counted potential cases in six states. That was not a complete audit of every irregularity, but it is far from showing fraud large enough to change the outcome.
  • Classification: a claim that persisted without support, with proof promised for later. It is not labeled "moving the goalposts," because no earlier standard of evidence that Trump accepted and then replaced was found.
  • Uncertain: whether Trump believed his claims. The guide makes no judgment about that.

Putin (2022)

  • Original sources: the Kremlin transcript (note 19) and the text of the law (note 20).
  • Context: art. 207.3 covers "knowingly false information," with a 10–15 year range for grave consequences. Art. 280.3 covers "discrediting" and is criminal after a prior administrative penalty within a year (up to 3 years), or up to 5 years with grave consequences. RFE/RL documents fines and charges for antiwar expression. No statute bans the single word "war."
  • Classification: euphemism enforced by law. No later retreat from the label is documented.

Trump on El-Sayed (2026)

  • Original sources: the post as archived (note 24). Wording was confirmed against the archive, including "Dumocrats" and "America Hating."
  • Evidence in the post: an asserted friendship with Piker and the word "Jihad." Nothing else.
  • Outside controversy:
    • The Piker appearances (note 25).
    • The Temple Israel statement, which contained both a condemnation and a cycle-of-violence explanation (note 27).
    • The apology, as quoted by PolitiFact (note 28). PolitiFact also rated Mike Rogers's claim that El-Sayed "could not condemn" the attack Mostly False.
    • A critical report alleging El-Sayed endorsed a 2012 statement supporting Egypt's then-president Mohamed Morsi was flagged by the review and has not been independently confirmed here. It is not used.
  • Classification: a sweeping label drawn from association that the post doesn't substantiate. The guide does not judge whether criticism of El-Sayed is prejudiced. No measurement of the post's reach or of who believed it was found, so the guide treats Trump's audience size as context, not proof.
  • Earlier post: an Oct. 6 post uses the phrase "Dumocrat Jihadists like El-Sayed" (archive). The main guide now mentions it in one sentence (note 24). It shows repeated, related labeling over two days. It does not show a return to a claim after a clarification, because no clarification was made.
  • Uncertain: whether a clarification will follow (none had been found by Oct. 8).

Historical and translation notes

  • Thucydides' narrative covers events of 427 BCE. He wrote it later.
  • In Tacitus, falsis nominibus goes with imperium ("they call it empire under false names"), not with the desert-and-peace clause. Edition 1 got this wrong.
  • In Legge's translation of 13.3 the line reads "proprieties and music do not flourish." The guide paraphrases it rather than quoting.

Revision Ledger

1. No boundary between manipulation and bullying. Resolved. Added a working definition and four separate questions, renamed the techniques "warning signs," and made "We don't have enough evidence to know" an explicit allowed answer. The definition treats evasion as possible, not necessary.

2. Trump on ISIS left out counterevidence. Resolved. The guide now quotes the policy argument he made in the same interview and describes the sequence (founder, policy, sarcasm) without judging his intent.

3. Biden misclassified as motte-and-bailey. Resolved. He is now a boundary case, the 12 are described as "individuals," and the CCDH dispute is noted. The term "motte-and-bailey" was dropped entirely.

4. Election example didn't show the goalposts moving. Resolved. It is reclassified as a claim that persists without support while proof is promised later. The "potential cases" qualifier and the review's scope are now included.

5. History asserted a shared theory. Resolved. The traditions are presented as distinct, both readings of Thucydides are given, Confucius is tied to roles, ritual and order, and the line equating Putin with Corcyra is gone.

6. McCarthy "never produced a list." Resolved. The guide now says he submitted names but did not substantiate his advertised accusations, citing the State Department memo. The memo's date, Feb. 16, 1953, is confirmed in the source.

7. El-Sayed overstated. Resolved. The post's words, its evidence, the outside controversy, El-Sayed's condemnation and apology, and the guide's conclusion are now separated. The no-plans statement is cited to Semafor (Aug. 26). The claims about the post's reach and about silence being treated as guilt are removed. Source correction: Dawud Walid's "political tactic" comment concerned a different August post using El-Sayed's full name (WDIV, Aug. 10, 2026). It is excluded as a response to the October post.

8. Legal paragraph implied blanket protection for opinion. Resolved. Added Milkovich, defined actual malice in plain terms, and stated that the guide does not rule on any specific accusation.
