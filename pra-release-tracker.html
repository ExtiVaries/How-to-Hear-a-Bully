Presidential Records Release & Extension Tracker

An evidence-based tracker for the Practical Guides collection. Research snapshot: October 27, 2026. This is a selected set of documented notices, not a national census or a measure of how frequently presidents use extensions.

This tracker follows what happened after the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) formally proposed opening presidential or vice-presidential records. It separates a routine statutory review extension from executive privilege, restricted material, court proceedings, and the later event of an actual release. The relevant unit is generally a PRA notification, which can cover several FOIA requests—not one extension per FOIA case.

How this process works

  • 44 U.S.C. § 2208(a)(3) generally gives the incumbent and former president 60 working days after NARA's notification to review proposed releases. Either may invoke one extension of no more than 30 additional working days by filing a written statement that additional review is needed. 36 C.F.R. § 1270.48(c) implements that rule.
  • The notice is public, but seeing standard language about possible extensions in a notice does not prove anyone invoked one. An extension is not, by itself, an executive-privilege claim, a withholding decision, or misconduct. A proposed date is not proof that records were delivered.
  • This PRA review extension is separate from a federal agency's ordinary FOIA response-time extension for unusual circumstances under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(B). Do not combine them into one statistic. Nor should a court scheduling order be counted as a PRA extension.

PA 2026-099 — January 6 material

Original notice: NARA's July 23, 2026 notification and Attachment A. The notice contains additional subjects beyond January 6. Three specifically relevant FOIA matters are:

  • 26-19335-F — Cassidy Hutchinson SMS messages from January 6, 2021: 12 electronic assets proposed for opening in full, with zero assets listed as restricted.
  • 26-19366-F — Christopher Liddell SMS messages from January 6, 2021: six electronic assets; one proposed for opening in full and five in part.
  • 26-19478-F — January 6 White House visitor logs: 140 WAVES electronic assets, all proposed for opening in part.

Chronology: NARA's July 23 letter gave October 19, 2026 as the proposed opening date, subject to a one-time extension or privilege claim. On October 27, 2026, Virginia Butler, a supervisory archivist at the Trump Presidential Library, replied in writing to an inquiry about this notice and these three case numbers that the White House had claimed an extension and the new proposed opening date was December 2, 2026.

Evidence accessibility: The original NARA notice is publicly linked above. The October 27 confirmation came from direct official correspondence held by the editor; it is not a publicly posted NARA extension filing. The signed extension statement, precise date of filing, and case-by-case scope were not independently retrieved. The correspondence did not confirm release, an executive-privilege assertion, or a court order. Do not imply that each of the three FOIA cases received a separate extension.

Next verification: Check whether NARA publishes the extension statement or a new notice, and separately seek evidence of release to original requesters versus public posting. Continue checking after December 2 if no release is confirmed.

LW 2024-084 — Biden vice-presidential material

Original notice: NARA's June 27, 2024 letter proposed opening material from two FOIA requests on September 23, 2024, including correspondence connected to Hunter and James Biden. The letter described 350 processed emails, with 105 proposed for full opening and 101 for partial opening. Those quantities were proposed, not a verified publication total.

Extension confirmation: In America First Legal Foundation v. National Archives and Records Administration, D.D.C. No. 1:22-cv-02713, ECF 25, filed September 30, 2024, a joint status report stated that the 30-working-day extension had been invoked for NARA's June 27 notification. Read the filed report (PDF copy hosted by a litigant; page 2).

Evidence limits: The joint filing confirms use of the extension, but its passage does not identify which individual representative invoked it, establish any motive, prove a privilege claim, or document eventual public release. One party's later public characterization of why the extension was used is not equivalent to a court finding. This is a historical comparison case, not a conclusion about the merits of the underlying requests.

Can we find every time an extension was used?

Not directly from the public notices alone. NARA's PRA notification index is an excellent directory of initial proposed releases; it is not presented as a complete, searchable log of all later extension statements. NARA's FOIA tracking guidance explains how request status is checked, but that is not a comprehensive historical extension database either.

A defensible audit would:

  1. List notices by notice ID, presidency/vice presidency, issuance date, original proposed opening date, and individual FOIA case IDs, without counting notice boilerplate as an extension.
  2. Seek actual extension statements and associated metadata from NARA: requestor (incumbent/former president or authorized representative), date received, affected notice and records, and adjusted proposed date. These could be requested from NARA as administrative records under FOIA; exemptions and records availability may limit disclosure. Ask for a structured list if an existing one is maintained, without assuming one exists.
  3. Cross-check federal dockets where FOIA requesters have sued and contemporaneous direct NARA correspondence or requester disclosures. Label litigation documents by their actual evidentiary role.
  4. Confirm outcomes separately through the relevant presidential library, a requester's documented receipt, a published FOIA production, or a court order. Late publication by itself does not establish why a release was delayed.
  5. Preserve unknowns and corrections. For any later quantitative comparison, first specify the covered time period, notice population, missing-data rate, and how multiple FOIA cases within one notice were deduplicated.

Fields, status rules, and corrections

The machine-readable event register stores the notice identifier, material, original date, confirmation date, source type, invoking authority when confirmed, revised proposed date, release/privilege status, and verification limits. Add a new record only with attributable evidence. Append dated corrections instead of silently erasing prior assessments.

Labels used: Proposed = records identified for opening, not delivered; extension confirmed = reliable evidence an extension was invoked; privilege claimed = separately documented assertion; released to requester = delivery verified but not necessarily publicly posted; publicly published = inspectable access link; unknown = the available evidence does not establish the event.

Editorial boundaries: The purpose is to make public-records process visible, not to infer hidden motivations, accuse officials of unlawful conduct, or suggest that every delay has the same cause. Verify official records across administrations by the same rules. This research has not audited all past NARA notifications, nor requested a complete set of extension statements.
