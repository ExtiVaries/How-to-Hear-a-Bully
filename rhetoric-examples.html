Rhetoric in Practice

Verified examples · Started October 10, 2026

A companion tracker to How to Hear a Bully. An entry is not a verdict that a speaker is a bully or intended to deceive. We separate what was said, the context, the evidence, and uncertainty.

October 8, 2026 — JD Vance defines “socialism”

Speaker and setting: Vice President JD Vance, at a press conference on employment-visa enforcement. A reporter asked him to define socialism personally.

What he said: Vance acknowledged that socialism was traditionally associated with prioritizing workers, said he did not know what the term meant anymore, then described its meaning in the American context as replacing American workers with foreign workers and allowing boys to play in girls' sports. He linked that description to his criticism of self-described socialists opposing visa restrictions.

Why it matters: This shifts an economic label, ordinarily centered on social ownership or control of productive resources, toward immigration and transgender sports participation. It is an example of changing moral and political labels.

Limits: The remarks do not establish his private beliefs, deliberate deception, or bullying by themselves. His visa-policy criticisms require separate examination. Short circulating clips omit his opening acknowledgement of socialism's traditional association with workers.

Ask: “Are you defining socialism as an economic system, or describing policies you associate with politicians who call themselves socialists?”

October 9, 2026 — Markwayne Mullin redirects a question about deportation aircraft

Speaker and setting: Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, at a press conference about the ICE shooting of Oscar Belgal in New York City.

What happened: Mullin denied that ICE was increasing arrests ahead of the midterms. When a reporter then asked whether DHS would keep using deportation aircraft purchased under the previous administration, he declined to answer, criticized “fake news,” and suggested the reporter was defending someone DHS considered dangerous.

Why it matters: This is an example of questioning the questioner's loyalties instead of addressing the question. Asking about government aircraft does not itself imply support for the person arrested.

Alternative reading and limits: Mullin could reasonably prefer questions about the shooting at a briefing devoted to that incident. He did answer the separate surge question with a denial. The exchange does not establish his private intentions, whether the aircraft were luxury planes, or whether the reported arrest directive existed.

Ask: “Can you answer the question about the aircraft without assuming that asking it means defending the person who was arrested?”

Original context: CNN press-conference transcript · Reuters reporting.

October 10, 2026 — Mike Rogers endorses a “terrorist” label in a football dispute

Speaker and setting: Mike Rogers, Republican nominee for U.S. Senate in Michigan, reposting a message by sports commentator Dave Portnoy about Democratic opponent Abdul El-Sayed.

What happened: El-Sayed, a University of Michigan graduate, joked about whether the school's football team could win without cheating. Portnoy posted the clip and wrote, “This guy really is a terrorist.” Rogers reposted it, adding, “@StoolPresidente is exactly right.” Asked whether Rogers meant this literally, his spokeswoman answered with a football joke about Ohio State talking points rather than a direct yes or no.

Why it matters: This illustrates amplifying a loaded label while leaving its intended meaning open. The video concerns sports banter, not evidence of terrorism. “Terrorist” usually implies involvement in political violence. Rogers had also raised allegations involving El-Sayed's family and terrorist organizations at their October 8 debate; contemporary reporting did not find evidence that El-Sayed personally supported those groups.

Alternative reading and limits: Portnoy's remark is plausibly football hyperbole, and Rogers's spokeswoman highlighted that context. The posts do not establish that either man intended a literal accusation, intimidation, or unlawful incitement. The earlier debate's disputed claims require their own assessment. The same test applies to public figures of every affiliation.

Ask: “Was your agreement only football banter, or were you alleging that your opponent is actually involved in terrorism? If the latter, what evidence supports that?”

October 11, 2026 — Donald Trump announces an energy ceasefire

Speaker and setting: U.S. President Donald Trump, in a Truth Social post.

What he said: On October 11, 2026, Donald Trump announced an immediately effective energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine and wrote, 'Both parties have agreed.' His wording presented mutual agreement and an immediate start as settled. The public responses examined here did not establish the same certainty about the arrangement's terms and status.

Context: Ukraine welcomed the announcement and said it was ready to refrain from striking Russian energy infrastructure. It also sought details of the American–Russian dialogue and expected reciprocal Russian restraint. On October 12 and 13, Interfax reported that Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said contacts were continuing.

Editorial interpretation, alternative reading and limits: The useful distinction is between a confident announcement and what listeners can verify. These sources do not establish what private assurances Trump had received, whether he knowingly misstated anything, or whether every relevant term remained unresolved. Ukraine's welcome and stated readiness are important counterevidence to any suggestion that both parties rejected the proposal. Reports of subsequent attacks raise a separate question about scope and compliance; attacks alone would not prove that no agreement existed.

Practical question: What has each side confirmed, which facilities are covered, when does the agreement begin, and how will compliance be checked?

Apply the same standard: Apply the same questions to a leader you support. This statement alone does not establish bullying or a pattern of manipulation.

How entries are selected

Each new entry should provide a source, context, a careful explanation of the mechanism, counterevidence or alternative readings, and a practical question. Unsupported examples are not presented as verified. Significant corrections should be documented rather than silently rewritten.
