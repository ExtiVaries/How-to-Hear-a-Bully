Who to Trust When It All Breaks Down? Source Notes

Back to the guide · Editorial review: 9 October 2026

A supplement to the guide. Numbers match the bracketed markers in the guide; each note links back to the place it is cited. Sources were checked on 9 October 2026. Where a source could not be retrieved in full, the note says so.

How to read these notes: each note names the kind of evidence (observational study, field study, randomized experiment, committee guidance, professional standard) and the limit that matters most. "Inspected" means the text was read, not that the data were reanalyzed. The five questions, the stopping rule and both exercises are the authors' teaching material. No source below tested them, and they have not been tried with readers. Notes 1 and 2 share investigators but examine different groups and tasks. Notes 5 and 6 are guidance from the same institution. These sources do not jointly validate our five questions.

1. What professional fact-checkers did

Wineburg, S., & McGrew, S. (2019). "Lateral reading and the nature of expertise: Reading less and learning more when evaluating digital information." Teachers College Record, 121(11), 1–40. DOI

  • Kind: Descriptive think-aloud comparison of selected groups. Publisher's abstract inspected; full text not retrieved.
  • Design: 45 experienced internet users (10 professional fact-checkers, 10 PhD historians, 25 Stanford undergraduates) evaluated live websites while thinking aloud. The paper analyzes three of the tasks.
  • Supports: Fact-checkers left unfamiliar sites after a quick scan and used other tabs to judge the original site ("lateral reading"). They reached more warranted conclusions than the other groups in a fraction of the time. Historians and students more often stayed on the site and were misled by features such as logos and domain names.
  • Limits: Small, purposive groups; not randomized and not a population estimate. It identifies a useful practice of trained professionals; it does not show that every fact-checker judgment was right, or that a short guide transfers their expertise. No effect sizes or scoring details are used here because the full text was not inspected.

2. Teaching students to investigate outside a page

Wineburg, S., Breakstone, J., McGrew, S., Smith, M. D., & Ortega, T. (2022). "Lateral reading on the open internet: A district-wide field study in high school government classes." Journal of Educational Psychology, 114(5), 893–909. DOI · ERIC abstract record · Author-project summary

  • Kind: Classroom field study in one urban US school district. ERIC abstract record, author-project summary and a Stanford news release inspected by the drafter; full paper not retrieved.
  • Design: 271 students in treatment classrooms, whose teachers received professional development and taught six 50-minute lessons in a required government course, were compared with 228 students in regular classrooms.
  • Supports: Students in the treatment classrooms "grew significantly in their ability to judge the credibility of digital content" (ERIC abstract).
  • Limits: High school students, one district, one curriculum and researcher-designed assessment tasks; this does not establish lasting change in adults' everyday decisions. Six lessons is far more instruction than reading a short guide. Allocation not verified: sources describe the design differently. The final abstract on ERIC describes "a matched control design"; the author-project summary describes a "cluster-randomized" analysis; the Stanford release calls it a "randomized experiment." Matching and random assignment can coexist, so this is an unverified detail, not a demonstrated contradiction. The independent review reports that the SSRN abstract combines both descriptions and that the APA supplement does not settle allocation; the drafter did not inspect either. The guide therefore describes a comparison with classes taught as usual and neither calls the study randomized nor nonrandomized. The SSRN abstract gives six one-hour lessons; the final abstract's 50 minutes is used here. No effect sizes are used.

3. Searching can raise belief in false stories

Aslett, K., Sanderson, Z., Godel, W., Persily, N., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. A. (2024). "Online searches to evaluate misinformation can increase its perceived veracity." Nature, 625, 548–556. Published online 20 December 2023. DOI · Author-institution PDF

  • Kind: Five survey experiments with US online samples. Author-institution PDF inspected by the drafter (abstract, designs, results and stated limitations), through a tool that summarizes and quotes short passages; publisher page did not load here. The initial independent review checked the PubMed abstract. The final check independently verified the numerical estimates and agreement wording in search-indexed passages from the original article and author-institution PDF; it did not retrieve the complete PDF or verify its page layout.
  • Design: Studies 1 and 5 randomly assigned some participants to search before rating articles; studies 2–4 asked the same people to rate an article, search, then rate again. Article truth was set by professional fact-checkers. Study 5 used a browser plug-in to record the search results participants saw, and rated those results' sources with NewsGuard scores.
  • Supports: Across the five studies, being prompted to search raised the probability of rating a false or misleading article as true by 0.057 to 0.107, roughly 6–11 percentage points (Results, "Measuring the online search effect" and "Unreliable results affect misinformation belief"). Some alternative rating scales did not show a significant change. In study 5, the effect was concentrated among people whose results included more unreliable sources.
  • Limits: In studies 1 and 5, the prompt to search was randomly assigned; in studies 2–4, the same people rated before and after searching. In no study was the quality of results people saw randomly assigned, and the authors say the quality analysis does not establish causation. Study 5 used a non-representative Mechanical Turk sample with uneven data loss between groups, and NewsGuard ratings were an August 2021 snapshot. The authors describe fact-checkers' agreement on article truth as "quite low" (Methods, "Determining the veracity of articles"). The study tested a simple instruction to search, not trained lateral reading; it does not show that every search misleads. A search for corrections on 9 October 2026 found none; that is not proof none exists.

4. Brief training for adults: small and uncertain differences

Oswald, L., Kozyreva, A., Herzog, S. M., Nickl, P. L., & Hertwig, R. (2026). "Boosting media literacy using lateral reading and online search interventions." Psychological Science, 37(8), 527–539. DOI · Publisher full text · Official society abstract · PubMed record

  • Kind: Randomized controlled trial. The drafter inspected only the official society abstract. The independent review inspected the full publisher text, including methods and results, which confirms the small initial differences, not all statistically clear, and improved control-group performance at follow-up. The page range is confirmed by the PubMed record, per the review.
  • Design: The abstract describes "a nationally representative randomized controlled study among adults in Germany (N = 2,666)," comparing two different interventions, one source-focused (lateral reading) and one claim-focused (online search), with a control.
  • Supports: "Both interventions showed small improvements in discernment, but not all effects were statistically distinguishable from zero." At two weeks, "discernment improved in all groups," and differences between intervention and control groups "were no longer statistically distinguishable." The authors report no evidence of backfire.
  • Limits: Not statistically distinguishable is not the same as no effect. Because the control group also improved by two weeks, a later difference that is not statistically clear does not by itself show a decline or a lack of lasting effect; the guide says only that differences from the comparison group "were no longer statistically clear." This result limits promises of durable benefit from brief instruction; it does not contradict note 2, which tested much longer instruction in a different population.

5. Weighing a body of evidence, not a single study

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2019). Reproducibility and Replicability in Science, chapter 7, "Confidence in Science," pp. 143–162. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press. DOI · Official chapter

  • Kind: Consensus committee report. Chapter text and Recommendation 7-3 inspected online by the drafter. Recommendation 7-3 is on p. 161, as located by the independent review in the online chapter's printed-page boundaries; the drafter's tool could not map pages.
  • Supports: Recommendation 7-3 says decision-makers should be "wary of making a serious decision based on the results, no matter how promising, of a single study," and that "no one should take a new, single contrary study as refutation of scientific conclusions supported by multiple lines of previous evidence." The chapter describes research synthesis as a widely accepted way to judge bodies of research.
  • Limits: Committee guidance, not an experiment that validates any reader method. It does not say a single decisive study can never overturn a conclusion, or that every review is sound. The guide's advice to use a current review that identifies its authors and evidence and explains its assessment is the authors' adaptation.

6. Limits of expertise, visible dissent, and community knowledge

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2026). "Earning and Maintaining Trust." In On Being a Scientist: A Guide to the Responsible and Ethical Conduct of Research (4th ed., version 1.0, updated September 2026). Version DOI · Official module

  • Kind: Professional ethics guidance. Module text and version date inspected. The version DOI resolves to the v1.0 module page.
  • Supports: Epistemic humility involves "acknowledging what is not known, recognizing the limits of expertise, avoiding overconfident claims." "Scientific debates should be visible in science communication," including minority dissent on key evidence. Researchers should not "present advocacy positions as if they were neutral scientific assessments"; the module treats both analysis and advocacy as legitimate, provided the role being played is clear. "Consensus is only trustworthy if it is properly formed." Respectful engagement means "recognizing that community members have expertise that researchers do not possess."
  • Limits: Guidance addressed to researchers about how they should behave, informed by scholarship; it is not evidence that any given scientist follows it, and its discussion of trust does not establish accuracy. Turning it into questions a reader asks of sources is the authors' adaptation. The module's historical cases were not checked and are not used.

7. Disclosing funders' roles and specifying corrections

International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (updated January 2026). Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing, and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals, sections II.B and III.A (corrections, p. 8). Official PDF · Conflicts of interest section

  • Kind: Professional publishing standard. Dated PDF and conflicts-of-interest web section inspected.
  • Supports: A potential conflict arises when judgment about a primary interest "may be influenced by a secondary interest (such as financial gain)"; perceived conflicts are treated as important. Neither a perceived nor an actual conflict proves a claim false. Authors should describe sponsors' roles in study design and any restrictions on publication, or state that there were none. "Honest errors are a part of science"; corrections should come with a new version and "details of the changes"; errors "serious enough to invalidate a paper's results and conclusions may require retraction"; "matters of debate and evolving science and methods are not errors."
  • Limits: Written for medical journals; it does not show that any journal or author complies, and it is not a general credibility test. Applying the same questions to civic information is the authors' adaptation. Disclosure does not eliminate a conflict, and a correction neither proves general reliability nor shows dishonesty.
